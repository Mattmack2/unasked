# The worm grew a new brain. Did the memory survive?

Planarian flatworms can regenerate a head after decapitation, including new cephalic ganglia. That makes them one of the few animals in which researchers can ask a normally impossible question: **can an experience acquired before brain removal still affect behavior after a new brain is built?**

Modern experiments say maybe. They do not yet tell us where the information was stored.

The first important correction is anatomical. A posterior planarian fragment can lose the original brain while retaining its two ventral nerve cords and peripheral sensory circuitry. `New brain` therefore does not mean `entirely new nervous system`.

That matters because the most provocative interpretation — memory stored in muscle, stem cells, RNA or a body-wide physiological field — is not required by the evidence. Surviving neural tissue could itself carry a training-induced change and later influence the regenerated brain.

A widely cited 2013 experiment already contained this ambiguity. Worms were familiarized with a textured feeding environment, decapitated, and allowed to regenerate. When tested directly after regeneration, the previously familiarized group performed slightly better than controls, but **not significantly**. The positive result appeared only after both groups received one additional three-hour exposure to the environment. Four days later, previously trained worms reached food faster: a classic `savings` effect.

So something about earlier experience may have made relearning easier. That is weaker than an intact memory simply reappearing in the regenerated brain.

A peer-reviewed 2026 study produced a more direct result using conditioned place preference. Girardia flatworms normally preferred a rough surface. Researchers repeatedly paired the initially non-preferred smooth surface with 10% sucrose, and trained worms later reversed their preference.

They then tested whether that altered preference survived regeneration. The decisive cohort consisted of **six sucrose-trained worms**. Each was cut into a head piece and a tail piece; after seven days of regeneration, animals from both pieces preferred the previously sucrose-associated smooth surface. For tail-derived regenerates — the important group because the original brain was absent — the multiple-comparison-adjusted p-value was **0.0480**.

That result is worth taking seriously. It is also much less definitive than the abstract makes it sound.

The regeneration cohort is tiny, the tail result sits just inside the conventional significance threshold, and the experiment did not include the cleanest control for associative learning: animals receiving the same sucrose exposure without sucrose reliably predicting one surface. Without that kind of unpaired or yoked control, a persistent shift in texture valuation, sensory responsiveness or another non-associative effect is harder to exclude.

The dopamine experiment in the same paper does not solve the storage problem. Blocking D1 receptors during training prevented the sucrose-associated preference shift. That shows dopamine signaling matters for producing the behavioral effect under this protocol; it does not reveal where the relevant state later resides.

Then comes the uncomfortable replication evidence.

A separate Harvard/Gershman group reported in 2026 that it could not reproduce classical planarian conditioning despite trying historical light-shock methods, several alternative sensory pairings, different worm sources, paired versus pseudoconditioned controls, and automated computer-vision analysis. In one newer fear-conditioning-style replication they used 48 conditioned and 12 pseudoconditioned worms and still found no learning effect.

They also quantified a problem that haunted the old literature: different observers often disagreed about what the worms were doing. In a scoring audit, all three observers agreed on only **28.15% of contraction events** and **15.14% of turns**.

That study does not refute the sucrose place-preference experiment, because it tested different learning paradigms. It does change the burden of proof. Before asking where a memory survives decapitation, the field needs a learning assay that independent laboratories can reliably produce before decapitation.

The controversy is active rather than historical. An August 2026 Levin-group preprint reports another positive result using light paired with food: most regenerated animals reportedly retained the learned response after two weeks. It is a different task, which is useful, but it is only a preprint and has not yet resolved the replication problem.

The best question is therefore smaller than `where was the memory stored?`

It is:

**What is the smallest experience-dependent state that can survive removal of the original brain and still bias the regenerated animal?**

That question suggests a cleaner experimental ladder. First establish a reproducible learned effect with blinded automated scoring and proper unpaired controls. Then remove the brain. Then progressively disrupt surviving candidate substrates — ventral nerve cords, particular sensory circuits, molecular states or other tissues — and ask when the post-regeneration bias disappears.

If the effect survives destruction of the remaining nervous tissue, the non-neural storage hypotheses become much more interesting. If it disappears when the ventral nerve cords are disrupted, the mystery becomes less exotic but still important: a peripheral nervous system would be carrying enough history to shape a newly built brain.

For now, the durable result is not that flatworms have proven memory outside the brain. It is that regeneration gives us a rare causal experiment on **how much of an old organism must remain for past experience to matter to a new brain**.
