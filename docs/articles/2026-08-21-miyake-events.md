# A tree can date history more precisely than it records the sky

A rare radiocarbon surge can turn a piece of archaeological wood into something much closer to a calendar than ordinary radiocarbon dating allows.

That has already happened. Three worked pieces of wood from L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland contain the known radiocarbon anomaly around 993 CE. Researchers located the anomaly in each piece, counted the remaining annual rings to the preserved outer edge, and all three trees converged on a cutting year of **1021 CE**. The secure claim is Norse activity in Newfoundland that year—not necessarily the first arrival or founding of the settlement.

The same basic trick has helped place the Changbaishan/Paektu Millennium Eruption in **late 946 CE** and anchor a 303-year Neolithic tree-ring chronology from Dispilio in Greece so that its final ring falls in **5140 BCE**.

The first version of this story sounds almost too clean:

`solar particle event → radiocarbon spike → one marked tree ring → exact year`

Turn 2 made that model harder to defend without weakening the dating result.

The strongest 774 and 993 CE signals really are extraordinarily useful synchronization marks. A 2018 study measured 484 individual rings from 44 tree-ring records on five continents. The two excursions were coherent enough across that network to independently validate the annual placement of those chronologies. Other cosmogenic isotopes in polar ice, including beryllium-10 and chlorine-36, support an extreme solar-particle origin for those two flagship events.

But a tree is not an atmospheric sampling instrument.

Radiocarbon enters a ring only after the physical event has produced cosmogenic carbon, the atmosphere has transported and mixed it, the tree has photosynthesized during its particular growing season, and that carbon has been allocated into new wood. Some of the carbon used to build a ring was not even captured from the air that year.

A 2026 *New Phytologist* synthesis makes this visible in the published 774-event data. Ten larch series show an earlier rise in 774 and a peak in 775. Three oak series show the rise a year later and peak in 776. Evergreen conifers sit between them with a lower, broader peak.

Stored carbon provides part of the mechanism. Modern isotope experiments summarized in the same paper estimated that roughly **50% of the carbon in oak earlywood** and **25% in larch earlywood** could have been assimilated in previous years, while latewood in those species was formed mostly from current-year carbon. A tree can therefore carry yesterday's atmosphere into today's ring.

That creates an apparent paradox. How can an archive whose physical signal is biologically blurred still date an archaeological event to one year?

Because **synchronization and measurement are different problems**.

If the goal is to reconstruct the exact atmospheric pulse—when particle production began, how abruptly it rose, its seasonal timing and peak magnitude—then a one-year shift caused by stored carbon or phenology is serious. Earlywood versus latewood, species, latitude, atmospheric transport and carbon allocation can all matter.

If the goal is instead to place an unknown annual sequence on the calendar, the archive does not need to be a perfect atmospheric recorder. It needs a sufficiently distinctive and independently validated fingerprint. Once that fingerprint is matched to the correct part of a well-crossdated sequence, ordinary ring counting can carry the date to the archaeological target.

Dispilio shows how much work is hidden behind the phrase “exact-year dating.” Juniper can contain missing or false rings, so the researchers did not simply find one tall radiocarbon value and declare victory. They built a crossdated master chronology, used conventional radiocarbon first to narrow the search interval, then made **115 single-ring measurements across four separate juniper samples**. The event appeared in the same relative ring in all four. The study also crossdated the juniper and oak chronologies and explicitly considered missing-ring problems.

Changbaishan is similarly overconstrained. The 774/775 signal was found in a tree killed by the eruption and combined with Greenland ice-core evidence to place the eruption in late 946. A second buried tree later reproduced the radiocarbon anchor and again placed its outermost ring in 946.

The best counterexample came from another genuine cosmogenic event around 660 BCE. A 2024 study constrained its main episode to 664–663 BCE, but the tree-ring radiocarbon signal rose across multiple years. The authors explicitly warn against treating it as a straightforward single-year marker.

So “Miyake event” should not be mentally translated into “one universal spike ring.” Some events produce broader fingerprints, different species can smear them differently, and proposed events do not automatically become accepted chronological anchors. A claimed excursion around 3372–3371 BCE, for example, was not reproduced when researchers looked in independently dated bristlecone pine from California and oak from Europe.

That failed replication is part of why the method works when it works. The exactness is earned through agreement between constraints:

`replicated cosmogenic fingerprint`
`+ trustworthy annual crossdating`
`+ high-resolution radiocarbon measurements`
`+ ring counting to the target`
`= calendar anchor`

The useful mental-model change is not that tree rings have secretly become perfect timestamps. It is almost the opposite.

**A biological proxy can be imperfect as a measurement of the physical event and still be excellent as a synchronization marker.**

That distinction lets the same research program do two things at once: use extreme solar events to pin human history to calendar years, while becoming more cautious about what the exact shape of a radiocarbon spike says about the storm itself.
