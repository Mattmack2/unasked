# We cleaned up ship fuel. The clouds noticed.

The question started as a policy irony that sounded almost too neat to be useful:

> Ships stopped burning as much sulfur. The air got cleaner. The planet got a little warmer.

That is physically true. It is also the least interesting version of the story.

The more useful thing that happened in 2020 is that humanity accidentally ran a huge atmospheric experiment.

## The simple version

For decades, large ships burned sulfur-rich fuel. The exhaust produced sulfur dioxide, which forms sulfate aerosol particles in the atmosphere.

Over relatively clean ocean air, some of those particles become **cloud condensation nuclei**: tiny surfaces on which cloud droplets form. Add more particles and, under the right conditions, a cloud can end up with more numerous but smaller droplets.

Smaller droplets generally reflect more sunlight. Strong examples show up in satellite images as bright white lines called **ship tracks**.

Then, on 1 January 2020, the International Maritime Organization cut the permitted sulfur content of most marine fuel from 3.5% to 0.5%. IMO later reported that total sulfur-oxide emissions from shipping fell by about 70% in the first year. [IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/mediacentre/pressbriefings/pages/02-imo-2020.aspx)

The intended result was cleaner air and less damage to human health.

A side effect was that ships stopped brightening some clouds as strongly as before.

That removed a small amount of cooling that had been masking greenhouse warming.

## Why this is interesting

The first search made this look like a story about unintended consequences.

Cleaner air exposed warming. Fine.

But aerosol–cloud interactions are one of the awkward parts of climate science precisely because clouds are messy. Their brightness and coverage change constantly with humidity, temperature, winds and large-scale circulation. Even when you know aerosols matter, isolating the effect in the real atmosphere is difficult.

Shipping had already given researchers an unusually clean geometry: concentrated pollution plumes crossing relatively pristine ocean clouds.

Then the sulfur rule changed the strength of that plume almost overnight.

So the deeper question became:

**If we deliberately remove a major aerosol source, can we watch the cloud system respond?**

The answer appears to be yes—but not nearly as cleanly as the white lines in satellite photographs suggest.

## Where it becomes tangible

A 2024 study using four global climate models estimated that reducing shipping sulfur by roughly 80% produced a positive effective radiative forcing between **0.057 and 0.089 watts per square metre**. The mean was 0.073 W/m². [Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics, 2024](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/24/13361/2024/)

A different 2025 analysis used machine learning to estimate what marine clouds would probably have looked like without the sulfur rule. It got an almost identical number: **+0.074 ± 0.005 W/m²** for the low-cloud shortwave effect across three major shipping regions. [Communications Earth & Environment, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01911-9)

That agreement is encouraging.

Then comes the part that makes atmospheric science feel less cooperative: the researchers still found the signal **hard to detect** against ordinary cloud variability.

They knew a large emissions intervention had happened. They had satellite observations. They could model a counterfactual. And the clouds were noisy enough that the perturbation still struggled to stand out cleanly.

That is a useful fact on its own.

## The visible ship tracks turned out not to be the main thing

At first, the bright lines look like the obvious measurement target.

They are visually satisfying. A ship emits aerosol; a white line appears behind it. Cause and effect seem almost diagrammatic.

But a 2025 closure study in the southeastern Atlantic complicated that picture. The authors compared several ways of estimating the aerosol indirect forcing from ships and concluded that **detectable ship tracks represented only about 5% of the total forcing in their analysis**. Most of the effect appeared to come from aerosol that had spread out enough to change cloud properties without creating a crisp visible line. [Communications Earth & Environment, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-025-02825-w)

The 5% number should not be promoted into a global constant; it is a regional result from one closure study.

But the conceptual correction is important.

The dramatic evidence can be the biased evidence.

A cloud does not need to draw a white stripe on a satellite image for a ship plume to matter.

That changed the shape of the question. Instead of asking how many ship tracks disappeared, the more interesting problem became how much diffuse cloud perturbation disappeared with them.

## Then the lightning changed

The oddest branch in the research was not low-cloud brightness at all.

For years, researchers had noticed unusually high lightning activity along heavily trafficked tropical shipping corridors. One proposed mechanism was that sulfate aerosol changed the microphysics of deep convective clouds, not just the shallow clouds that produce visible tracks.

The 2020 sulfur rule gave that hypothesis a before-and-after test.

A 2025 study over the Bay of Bengal found that shipping-related sulfur emissions fell substantially after the rule. Satellite cloud observations showed changes consistent with weaker aerosol influence: larger droplets near cloud base, lower cloud tops and less ice water aloft. Lightning frequency during 2020–2023 was **11% lower than the pre-rule climatology**, and the authors estimated shipping had contributed at least 17% of climatological lightning in the studied region. [npj Climate and Atmospheric Science, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41612-025-01256-w)

That does not mean ships control global lightning.

It means the same aerosol perturbation showed up in a second, very different observable.

A white ship track and a lightning stroke are not the same measurement. Seeing both respond makes the natural experiment harder to dismiss as one satellite-retrieval curiosity.

## So did cleaner ship fuel cause the record heat of 2023?

This was where the first version of the story wanted to go.

It is also where the evidence becomes least satisfying.

Different modeling approaches estimate somewhat different forcing magnitudes. One UK Earth-system-model study estimated **0.139 ± 0.019 W/m²** and about **0.046°C** of global average warming across the 2020s. It concluded that IMO2020 probably helped explain some of 2023's exceptional warmth, but that other factors were still required. [Earth's Future, 2024](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2024EF005011)

Other researchers have found lower values. Gavin Schmidt argued in 2024 that shipping-sulfur cleanup probably contributes only a few hundredths of a degree globally and was very unlikely to explain anything close to the full 2023 anomaly. [Nature, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00816-z)

That disagreement initially looked like a problem for the article.

It turned out not to matter very much.

The scientifically durable claim is smaller:

**removing sulfate pollution caused a measurable positive forcing.**

Whether it supplied 5%, 15% or some other fraction of one extraordinary warm year is a separate attribution problem involving El Niño, ocean variability, greenhouse forcing, volcanic effects and other changes.

The natural experiment remains valuable even if that exact accounting takes years to settle.

## This is not a case for keeping dirty fuel

There is an easy bad inference here:

> If sulfur pollution cooled the planet, maybe cleaning it up was a mistake.

That confuses a temporary mask with the underlying disease.

Sulfur pollution damages lungs, contributes to premature deaths and causes acid deposition. The IMO rule was designed specifically to reduce those harms. Before implementation, an analysis submitted to IMO estimated that failing to tighten the sulfur limit could contribute to more than half a million additional premature deaths worldwide between 2020 and 2025. [IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/mediacentre/pressbriefings/pages/19-implementation-of-sulphur-2020-limit-.aspx)

Sulfate aerosol is also short-lived. Carbon dioxide is not.

If both are emitted together, the aerosol can temporarily hide some greenhouse warming. Remove the aerosol and that hidden warming appears quickly. The durable answer is to remove the harmful aerosol **and** reduce the long-lived greenhouse gas—not to preserve the pollution because it happens to scatter sunlight.

## The marine-cloud-brightening connection is real, but uncomfortable

Once the shipping experiment is framed this way, it naturally leads to **marine cloud brightening**: proposals to deliberately spray carefully sized sea-salt particles into susceptible marine clouds to reflect more sunlight.

Ships are not an operational prototype for that idea. Their exhaust contains sulfate and other combustion products, the particle-size distribution is uncontrolled, and the routes are determined by trade rather than climate optimization.

But the shipping record does answer one important question: can a large aerosol perturbation measurably alter marine clouds in the real world?

Yes.

It also supplies several reasons to be cautious.

Cloud susceptibility varies by meteorological state. Much of the forcing can be diffuse rather than visibly track-like. The signal can be hard to detect even when the intervention is known. And a 2024 Nature Climate Change study found that a regional cloud-brightening intervention that reduced heat risk under present climate conditions could become much less effective—or create remote warming effects—under a warmer background climate. [Nature Climate Change, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-024-02046-7)

So the shipping experiment is not a proof that cloud brightening works safely.

It is a reminder that clouds actually respond—and that controlling the response is a different problem from demonstrating that one exists.

## What this changed for me

The first version of this topic was basically a clever sentence: cleaning up pollution accidentally warmed the climate.

After chasing it down, that sentence feels like a distraction.

The stranger and more useful result is that a regulation written for air quality changed one of the atmosphere's inputs sharply enough to become a global scientific instrument.

Researchers watched the obvious white lines decline. Then they found that the obvious lines were probably only a fraction of the effect. They found cloud changes that were real but hard to separate from natural variability. Then a different group found the perturbation echoed in tropical lightning.

And the number I expected to matter most—how much of 2023's heat came from the sulfur cleanup—ended up mattering less.

The experiment is interesting precisely because the answer is not a single climate number.

We changed the aerosol source on purpose. The clouds reacted. And even with that unusually clean intervention, working out exactly how much energy moved through the climate system is still difficult.

That is a much better explanation of why aerosol–cloud interactions remain one of the stubborn parts of climate science.
