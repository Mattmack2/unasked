# Which version of the past does a smell belong to?

A museum can put a 200-year-old book in a climate-controlled case and reasonably say the object is still there.

Its smell is harder.

The molecules coming off that book today are partly the smell of paper, glue, leather and ink—and partly the smell of two centuries of chemical change. If we reproduce that odour perfectly, have we preserved the book's historical smell? Or only the smell of its ageing?

That awkward question sits at the center of a small but increasingly serious field called **olfactory heritage**.

## The simple version

Some smells matter historically because they are tied to a place, object, craft, ritual or way of life. Researchers are now trying to document those smells with chemistry, sensory testing, historical sources and, sometimes, physical reconstructions.

The surprising part is that a smell cannot usually be archived the way a photograph can.

A useful olfactory record may need all of these at once:

- the object or practice that produces the smell;
- measurements of the volatile chemicals it emits;
- evidence about which of those chemicals humans actually notice;
- descriptions from people who smell it;
- historical context;
- the formula or method used to reconstruct it;
- and a statement of **which moment in time** the reconstruction is supposed to represent.

The last item turns out to matter a lot.

## Why this is interesting

We are used to treating the past as mostly visual.

Photographs preserve streets. Recordings preserve voices. Buildings, paintings and tools can be conserved. But a tannery, old library, railway carriage, incense ritual, market or working stable could be recognizable partly because of its smell—and that part can vanish without leaving anything obvious behind.

The mistake is to imagine that the solution is simply “save the molecules.”

Smell is not a chemical parts list. A compound present at a tiny concentration can dominate perception if humans are especially sensitive to it. A compound present in abundance can matter surprisingly little. Mixtures interact. Familiarity and context affect what people notice and how they describe it.

So preserving a historically important smell is partly an analytical problem and partly an interpretation problem.

## Where it becomes tangible

In June 2026, researchers published a good demonstration of how far the analytical side has come.

They studied an early-20th-century perfume flask. Instead of merely running an ordinary chemical analysis and copying every detected compound, they used **gas chromatography with olfactory detection**: chemicals were separated instrumentally while human assessors identified which fractions actually carried noticeable odours.

The team then ranked compounds by sensory relevance, selected 13 important contributors, built a reconstruction, performed omission tests, and asked a mixed panel of 70 trained and untrained participants to compare it with the surviving perfume.

Under the experiment, the panel did not find a significant difference between the reconstruction and the historical sample. [npj Heritage Science, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-026-02699-x)

That sounds like a tiny form of time travel until you notice the most important sentence in the paper:

**the target was the perfume's current aged smell.**

The researchers were not claiming that they had recovered the original recipe or what the perfume smelled like when someone first opened the bottle a century ago.

That distinction is the whole subject in miniature.

## A smell can acquire a patina too

Conservators have dealt with a similar problem for a long time in other media.

Should an old painting look exactly as it did the day it left the studio? Should later varnish be removed? Does fading count as damage, history, or both? Can a repair become historically meaningful in its own right?

Conservation theory has spent decades arguing about original state, current state, patina, later alteration and authenticity.

Olfactory heritage takes that familiar problem and makes it volatile.

The characteristic smell of an old book, for example, is partly produced by degradation. Historic paper releases compounds as cellulose, lignin, adhesives and other materials age. Those emissions can carry useful information about the object's condition as well as creating the smell people associate with old libraries.

So there may be several legitimate things a museum could want to preserve:

1. what the object smells like **now**;
2. what it probably smelled like **when new**;
3. what the larger room or practice smelled like when people historically encountered it.

Those are different targets.

A perfect reconstruction of the first can be completely wrong for the second.

## This gets much messier when the original smell is already gone

Ancient Egyptian mummification is a good example.

Researchers can analyse residues inside vessels and identify evidence for oils, waxes, resins, bitumen and other materials. Museums can then work with perfumers to turn that chemistry into something visitors can actually smell.

But this is not a recovered sample of ancient air.

Modern ingredients are different. Some historical materials are unavailable or unsafe. Concentrations are uncertain. The residue itself has aged. And a perfumer has to make decisions wherever the evidence runs out.

A 2026 paper describing the museum project **Scent of the Afterlife** is unusually careful about this. The authors call the result a historically informed olfactory interpretation rather than a completely authentic recreation. [Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-archaeology/articles/10.3389/fearc.2025.1736875/full)

That phrasing is worth keeping. It lets a smell be useful without pretending the reconstruction is stronger evidence than it is.

## There is already a smell library

The European Odeuropa project has pushed this from experiments into something closer to infrastructure.

Its **Heritage Smell Library**, housed through the Osmothèque in France, began with 19 reference scents. Some represent single historically significant materials such as myrrh or frankincense. Others are reconstructions based on chemical evidence or recreations based on recipes and historical research. [Odeuropa](https://odeuropa.eu/the-heritage-smell-library/)

But the clever part is that Odeuropa does not treat the physical vial as enough.

The library also preserves documentation about what the smell represents, why it matters, how it was developed, and what evidence sits behind it.

That is probably the right archival unit.

A scent formulation can itself change over time. The raw materials used to remake it can change. A future researcher needs the **method and provenance**, not just a bottle labelled `OLD CAR`.

## Do people even smell the same thing?

Not exactly—but the answer is more interesting than “smell is subjective.”

One large cross-cultural study found substantial agreement in which simple odorants people considered pleasant, with molecular identity explaining far more variation than culture. Another study across very different populations also found broad commonalities, but stronger differences for some smells, with familiarity playing an important role. [Current Biology, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35381183/) [Royal Society Open Science, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38863390/)

So there appears to be a shared biological floor to olfaction, layered with individual experience, ecology, language and culture.

For heritage work, that means a sensory panel can tell us something useful about whether two stimuli are perceptually similar. It cannot tell us what that smell **meant** to everyone who encountered it.

The smell of incense can be chemically similar across two people while carrying completely different associations with family, worship, tourism, grief or home.

That cultural layer is part of the archive too.

## The most practical idea may be to stop waiting until things disappear

This was the part of the research that changed the question for me.

Trying to reconstruct a vanished smellscape after the fact is usually underdetermined. Once the local plant varieties, workshop process, market stalls, fuel, ventilation, weather patterns and living practitioners are gone, later researchers are forced to triangulate from whatever happened to survive.

UNESCO highlighted olfactory heritage in July 2026 with an example from Bahia's **Lavagem do Bonfim**, where aromatic herbs and waters are part of a living ritual tradition. Researchers are pairing chemical documentation with practitioners' own descriptions to establish a record of what the practice smells like today. [UNESCO, 2026](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/what-scent-our-past)

That creates a baseline.

If climate change alters plant availability, if ingredients are substituted, or if the knowledge stops being passed down in the same way, people in the future will have more than a photograph showing that the ceremony happened.

They will have evidence of one of the ways the ceremony occupied the air.

## What this could change

Olfactory heritage is unlikely to turn museums into giant perfume archives, and that would probably miss the point anyway.

The more useful shift is deciding that **sensory information deserves to be documented before it becomes invisible through absence**.

A historic workshop might be surveyed for sound, smell, temperature and material residues before conversion. A disappearing food process could be recorded through practitioner interviews plus headspace chemistry. A conservation team might deliberately preserve the odour of an object instead of treating it automatically as contamination to remove.

And reconstructions could carry better labels:

- measured current smell;
- reconstructed original formulation;
- historically informed recreation;
- interpretive smellscape.

Those labels sound fussy until you realize that without them, a convincing synthetic smell can quietly turn interpretation into false memory.

The interesting thing is not that chemistry has finally learned to bottle history.

It is that smell forces a more uncomfortable question about preservation in general:

**when something changes continuously through time, what exactly do we mean when we say we saved it?**

Sometimes the best answer may be to document the living version before anyone realizes it is about to become history.
