# Earth's “missing lead” may be partly a model problem

Lead isotopes let geochemists compare the Earth we can sample with the Earth a simple formation model predicts.

The comparison does not balance cleanly.

Uranium-238 and uranium-235 decay into lead-206 and lead-207. Lead-204 is not produced by radioactive decay, so ratios such as `206Pb/204Pb` record how long a reservoir spent with a particular uranium-to-lead ratio. Under a classical model in which the bulk silicate Earth began with a chondritic, meteorite-like composition, most accessible terrestrial rocks are **too radiogenic**.

That discrepancy is the **first lead paradox**. “Missing lead” does not mean Earth is simply short a known mass of Pb. It means the isotope history of the reservoirs we can sample lacks the complementary unradiogenic component required by the simple model.

There are real hints that such material exists. In 2008, researchers measured exceptionally unradiogenic Pb in mantle peridotites from Japan's Horoman massif. In 2012, a separate team found very unradiogenic Pb in sulfide inclusions inside abyssal mantle rocks. Those inclusions could remain poorly sampled by the partial melting that produces ordinary oceanic basalt. ([Nature Geoscience, 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo363); [2012](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo1531))

## What the 2026 result adds

A February 2026 *Nature Communications* paper asked what mineral could physically store old Pb deep in the mantle for billions of years while separating it from uranium and thorium.

Using first-principles structure prediction and melting calculations, the authors found that several lead-sulfur phases become stable under high pressure. Their calculations indicate that PbS can remain refractory through deep-mantle conditions; PbS2 and PbS3 have different pressure-dependent stability and melting behavior that could allow both long-term storage and limited remobilization. ([Nature Communications, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-69772-8))

That is a useful mechanism. It is not a detection.

The paper itself says specific Pb-hosting phases in the deep Earth have not been conclusively identified. Its hidden-reservoir analysis is also explicitly conducted **under the assumption of a chondritic bulk silicate Earth**.

The strongest counterargument attacks that premise rather than the mineral physics.

## The paradox gets smaller if the Earth model gets more complicated

A 2023 *Earth-Science Reviews* synthesis rebuilt global Pb-isotope evolution using modern datasets and more differentiated reservoir histories. Instead of treating the silicate Earth as one composition that later split cleanly into today's reservoirs, it modeled distinct Pb evolution paths, source mixing, gravitational differentiation and subduction-led remixing. ([Earth-Science Reviews, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012825223001721))

That framework does not make the observed isotope pattern disappear. It changes what the pattern demands.

If accessible rocks are preferentially sampling relatively radiogenic shallow reservoirs while less-radiogenic material follows different evolutionary paths, then every sample plotting to the radiogenic side of the meteorite geochron is not independent evidence for one enormous hidden vault of lead.

Turn 1 treated the 2026 Pb-S phases as a candidate solution to a missing reservoir. Turn 2 changed that conclusion: they are better understood as **a plausible storage mechanism within one family of explanations**, not “the solution to Earth's missing lead.”

Older work shows why that distinction is necessary. A 2008 partitioning experiment argued that Pb was not siderophile or chalcophile enough under the tested conditions for core sequestration alone to explain the mantle's Pb budget. Other models have defended stronger core partitioning under different conditions. Volatile loss during early Earth evolution has also been proposed. ([Nature, 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07375))

So several things can simultaneously be true:

- the classical Pb isotope imbalance is real;
- unradiogenic mantle components have actually been sampled;
- pressure-stable Pb-S minerals could preserve such components;
- and a better model of Earth's differentiation may reduce how much “missing” reservoir is required.

Isotope bookkeeping can show that a planetary model is incomplete long before the missing reservoir is directly identified. A new mineral-physics result can strengthen one explanation without settling the global history that made the explanation necessary.
