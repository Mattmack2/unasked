# What survives after the genome doesn't

A fossil tooth can be almost offensively uninformative. It is hard, mineralized, often isolated from the rest of the skeleton, and sometimes millions of years removed from anything we would recognize as biological tissue.

It can also still contain protein fragments from the animal that made it.

## The simple version

DNA is a spectacular historical record, but it is not the only molecular record inside a fossil.

Tooth enamel contains a small set of proteins laid down while a tooth forms. Enamel is overwhelmingly mineral, which is bad if you want a rich catalogue of biology but unexpectedly useful if you want a few molecules to survive for a very long time. Modern mass spectrometers can measure fragments of those proteins and sometimes reconstruct parts of their amino-acid sequences.

The result is a strange kind of archive: **much lower bandwidth than DNA, but in some fossils much deeper.**

Researchers are already using enamel proteins to infer biological sex, help place fossils on evolutionary trees, and identify inherited amino-acid variants in remains where useful DNA is absent or extraordinarily difficult to recover.

## Why this is interesting

The obvious mental model is that old fossils gradually lose information until only bones and shapes remain.

That is too simple.

Different kinds of biological information fail at different rates. DNA, collagen, enamel proteins, isotopes, tooth microstructure and morphology do not share one expiration date. The useful question is less “how old is the fossil?” than **“which archive could still be open in this specimen, under these preservation conditions?”**

That matters because molecular evidence can answer questions that shape alone often cannot. Two teeth can look similar while belonging to different lineages. A skeleton may not preserve enough anatomy for a confident sex estimate. A particular amino-acid variant can carry evolutionary information that is invisible to morphology.

Palaeoproteomics gives researchers another way in.

## Where it becomes tangible

In May 2026, researchers reported enamel proteins from six roughly 400,000-year-old *Homo erectus* specimens from three sites in China. The protein evidence supported sex assignments—five male, one female—and revealed two shared variants in ameloblastin, one of the proteins involved in enamel formation. One of those variants also occurs in Denisovans. The authors argue that this fits a possible history in which populations related to these *H. erectus* contributed ancestry to Denisovan populations. That is an interesting evolutionary clue; it is not the same thing as having a *H. erectus* genome or proving the full gene-flow history from one protein site. [Nature, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10478-8)

A different 2026 study examined 23 *Homo naledi* teeth representing at least 20 individuals. Using acid etching rather than cutting large pieces from the teeth, the researchers recovered enamel proteins and found no convincing male-specific markers in the analysed sample. They also found two amino-acid substitutions of evolutionary interest. The careful statement is “no confidently identified male markers,” not “everyone in the cave was female.” [Cell, 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0092867426006446)

And then there is the result that makes the time scale feel slightly ridiculous.

In 2025, a team working in Kenya's Turkana Basin reported fossil-derived enamel-protein fragments from rhinocerotid and proboscidean teeth about **16–18 million years old**. The site is especially interesting because warm environments are usually brutal on ancient biomolecules. [Nature, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09040-9)

But the details are better than the headline. More than 400 peptide matches initially passed the study's false-discovery threshold. Roughly 130 had top matches consistent with the sampled fossil groups. Only **19** were ultimately classified as high-confidence fossil-derived fragments under the authors' stricter criteria.

That narrowing is the story. The machine can detect a lot. The scientific work is deciding what deserves to survive the filter.

## There is no clean “DNA stops here” line

It is tempting to tell this as a relay race: DNA survives for a while, then proteins take over.

Nature is less tidy.

Exceptional DNA has survived for astonishing lengths of time. Environmental DNA recovered from Greenland sediment has been dated to roughly two million years, helped by very favorable preservation conditions. [Nature, 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05453-y) Nuclear DNA has also been recovered from roughly 430,000-year-old hominins at Sima de los Huesos in Spain. [Nature, 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17405)

So “proteins last longer than DNA” is not a law. Temperature matters. Burial chemistry matters. The substrate matters. Whether the molecule is sitting inside sediment, bone, dentine or enamel matters. What counts as “useful information” matters too.

The better comparison is bandwidth versus durability.

A genome can contain an enormous amount of hereditary information when it survives. An ancient enamel proteome might leave you with fragments from only a handful of proteins. Those fragments can still be enough to discriminate variants or support a phylogenetic placement, but they cannot answer everything a genome can.

Enamel is less like a backup copy of the genome and more like a few labels stamped into an unusually durable part of the machine.

## Why enamel is unusually good at this

Mature tooth enamel is the hardest tissue in the vertebrate body and is almost entirely mineral. During tooth formation, proteins such as amelogenin, enamelin and ameloblastin help build that structure. Some protein fragments become trapped inside it.

Over geological time they fragment, chemically modify and disappear. They do not sit pristine inside a tooth for eighteen million years. The analytical trick is to recover enough damaged pieces, recognize which are genuinely endogenous, and work out what can responsibly be inferred from them.

That last step is where palaeoproteomics gets difficult.

Reference databases are uneven: living species with sequenced genomes and well-characterized proteins are easier to compare against than extinct groups with no close sequenced relative. Some peptides are more likely to survive, extract, ionize or fragment in the mass spectrometer. Very old samples can leave only a few sequence positions that are actually informative.

And contamination can cause trouble without announcing itself.

A 2025 experiment deliberately contaminated Pleistocene rhinoceros enamel with modern protein. Under a typical enamel workflow, the researchers could not simply point to the contaminating proteins in the final results. Yet the contamination **reduced recovery of the genuine ancient proteome**, masking lower-abundance peptides and protein regions. The fix was mundane—careful decontamination, with simple water or bleach washes performing well in their tests—but the result is a useful warning. [Scientific Reports, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-31472-6)

Better measurement does not remove the need for boring controls.

## “Minimally destructive” still means destructive

There is another practical constraint that disappears in futuristic descriptions of the field: these fossils are not disposable samples.

Acid etching can dramatically reduce the amount of enamel sacrificed compared with drilling or cutting out a conventional sample. In the *H. naledi* work, it recovered useful protein information and may make preliminary screening of rare teeth more acceptable to collections.

It still changes the object.

Recent methods work has measured microscopic enamel loss from such approaches and points out that controlled etching can require removing a tooth from its socket. For a common archaeological sample that may be an acceptable trade. For a unique hominin fossil, the decision is different.

That means “can we measure this?” and “should we sample this specimen?” remain separate questions.

## The part I would watch next

Modern palaeoproteomics has quietly acquired something previous generations of fossil researchers did not have: a growing archive of **raw digital spectra**.

Major studies now deposit mass-spectrometry data in repositories such as PRIDE through ProteomeXchange. The 2026 *H. erectus* paper, for example, publishes its own raw data and points to multiple earlier hominin palaeoproteomic datasets for comparison. The field's major 2022 review explicitly argues that better reference databases, better knowledge of protein modifications and improved search methods should make reanalysis of old samples more useful over time. [Chemical Reviews, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9412968/)

There is a seductive version of this story where old spectra keep producing major discoveries as software improves. I could not verify that stronger claim in this research pass. I did not find a clean case where later reanalysis of the same ancient-enamel raw data materially overturned or upgraded an accepted historical conclusion.

So the current claim should stay smaller:

**saving the raw spectra preserves the possibility of asking better questions of the measurement later.**

That is already worth doing. It just is not yet evidence for a self-improving fossil archive.

## What this changes

The most useful consequence is probably not an 18-million-year-old record.

It is the possibility that museums contain large classes of fossils we have mentally categorized as **morphology only**, when some fraction of them actually retain another layer of evidence.

If sampling becomes gentler, screening becomes cheaper, reference databases improve, and authentication remains strict, researchers can start asking a different preliminary question when they encounter an old tooth:

> Before we decide what this fossil can tell us, which archives inside it have actually gone silent?

Sometimes the answer will still be all of them.

But increasingly, it will not.
