# A body can keep cells from another person for decades

The first version of this story looked almost sentimental: pregnancy leaves a permanent cellular scrapbook, with a few cells from each child lingering in the mother for life.

That story is partly true. It is also too neat.

## The simple version

During pregnancy, intact cells cross the placenta in both directions.

Some fetal-origin cells enter the mother. Some maternal-origin cells enter the fetus. Most disappear, but a very small number can persist for years or decades. The term for this is **microchimerism**: a tiny population of genetically distinct cells living inside another individual.

This is not the same as finding fragments of somebody else's DNA floating in blood. Classic studies found markers in sorted progenitor-like cells, and later work has found microchimeric material across multiple tissues.

The scale matters. These cells can be rarer than one in 100,000 host cells, sometimes one in a million or less depending on the tissue and assay. So the useful picture is not two bodies somehow sharing one organism. It is one organism with an extremely sparse cellular minority that came from somebody else.

And the exchange is reciprocal.

A mother can retain cells acquired during pregnancy. An adult child can retain maternal-origin cells acquired before birth.

## Why this is interesting

The odd part is not simply that foreign cells get across the placenta. Pregnancy is an unusually intimate biological interface; some leakage is not shocking.

The odd part is that some of those cells can remain for decades without being eliminated.

That complicates a very ordinary intuition: one body, one genome, one population of self cells. A human body is still overwhelmingly built from its own inherited genome, but its cellular membership is not perfectly genetically homogeneous.

I initially thought the best way to explain this was the scrapbook metaphor: each pregnancy leaves another tiny permanent layer.

Then the mouse literature broke it.

Experiments published in 2024 found that a new pregnancy could almost completely displace older microchimeric populations. Cells retained from a first pregnancy were largely replaced by cells associated with a later one; maternal-origin microchimeric cells already present in female mice were also sharply reduced after pregnancy. The effect still occurred in mice lacking B and T cells, so a simple classical adaptive-immune rejection story does not explain it.

That result does not show that human bodies reset their microchimeric populations the same way. It does make the permanent-scrapbook picture much harder to defend.

The better question is now: **what controls which rare foreign cells persist, where they persist, and when they disappear?**

## Where it becomes tangible

The evidence for long-term persistence is old enough to be reassuringly unglamorous.

In 1996, researchers studying fetal cells for prenatal diagnosis sorted blood cells from women who had previously given birth to sons. They detected Y-chromosome sequences in progenitor-like cell fractions in six of eight nonpregnant women. In one case, the relevant birth had occurred **27 years earlier**.

[Bianchi et al., PNAS 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8570620/)

Three years later, another group looked in the opposite direction. Using HLA-specific genetic markers, they found non-inherited maternal markers in immunocompetent children and adults. Positive subjects ranged up to age 49.

[Maloney et al., JCI 1999](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/6611)

Those studies are small by modern standards, and their methods have biases. The older fetal-cell literature in particular often used Y-chromosome markers because male-origin cells are convenient to distinguish from a female host. That makes male pregnancies much easier to study than female ones.

But the central result has held up: pregnancy can produce long-lived, naturally acquired microchimerism on both sides.

A 2012 autopsy study gives a more dramatic example. Researchers detected male microchimerism in 37 of 59 female brains, across multiple brain regions.

The tempting retelling is, “women can carry their sons' cells in their brains.” The paper does **not** establish that cleanly. Pregnancy histories were missing for most subjects, and male microchimerism can have other sources: an unrecognized vanished twin, an older male sibling transferred through the mother, or a prior transfusion. The study also did not show what those cells were doing.

That correction matters because this field has a persistent asymmetry: **presence is much easier to prove than function.**

## The function problem

Microchimeric cells have been associated with autoimmune disease, cancer, tissue repair, reproductive outcomes and transplant tolerance.

Nearly every one of those literatures contains a causality trap.

Suppose more fetal-origin cells are found in damaged maternal tissue. They might have contributed to the damage. They might have arrived because damaged tissue recruited them. They might be helping repair it. Or they might be passengers responding to the same signals as ordinary host cells.

A 2025 expert agenda involving 29 microchimerism researchers put methodology and mechanism near the center of the field's unresolved problems. These populations are so rare that laboratories can miss them, classify them differently or detect DNA without being able to say what cell type carried it.

[Chua et al., Advanced Science 2025](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/advs.202514969)

Cancer research shows the problem especially clearly. A 2025 review found conflicting associations across 20 studies and concluded there was no clear evidence for a causal role. A separate 2025 meta-analysis found lower pooled cancer risk among women with detectable male-origin microchimerism, but individual cancer types did not all point the same way. An association like that is interesting; it is not the same thing as discovering a protective mechanism.

## The older history made the story better

There is an unexpected genealogy here.

In 1945, geneticist Ray Owen was studying blood groups in fraternal cattle twins. Some twins carried two genetically distinct red-cell populations: their own and their sibling's. Because mature red cells do not persist indefinitely, Owen inferred that blood-forming precursor cells had crossed between the twins before birth, engrafted, and continued producing foreign blood cells later in life.

That observation became an important ancestor of **acquired immunological tolerance**. Burnet and Fenner incorporated it into their thinking about how immune systems distinguish self from non-self; Medawar's group later demonstrated inducible tolerance experimentally.

[Ray Owen and naturally acquired chimerism](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5063071/)

So the historical loop is slightly perverse. Natural chimerism helped immunology learn that organisms can tolerate genetically foreign cells. The later success of the self/non-self framework then made persistent foreign cellular populations feel like exceptions to explain away.

Microchimerism is not proof that individuality is an illusion. A person still has one dominant developmental organism, integrated physiology and overwhelmingly host-derived tissues.

It does show that the anatomical edge of a body is not a perfect boundary around one cellular genome.

## What this could change

The near-term shift is methodological rather than medical.

Older studies often answered a crude question: can we detect a non-host genetic marker at all? Better rare-cell methods increasingly make harder questions possible: what kind of cell is it, where is it physically located, which genes is it expressing, how old is that lineage, and what is happening around it?

That matters because the most interesting current hypothesis is now about **population dynamics**, not mere persistence.

An evolutionary paper published online in August 2026 takes the mouse displacement result seriously enough to model competing explanations. One model treats diversity of microchimeric cells as useful for tolerance; another treats fetal-origin lineages as potentially having evolutionary interests that do not perfectly match the mother's. The conflict model can generate replacement of older populations by newer ones.

[Úbeda & Wild, Journal of Evolutionary Biology 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jeb/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jeb/voag071/8761893)

That is theory built on animal evidence. It is not demonstrated cellular warfare inside human mothers.

But it points toward a better frontier than the one I started with. The established discovery was that cells can remain after pregnancy. The harder problem is beginning to look like this:

**A body can tolerate a few cells from another person for decades. We still do not know whether those cells are passive remnants, useful residents, context-dependent troublemakers, or members of a rare population whose composition is actively regulated.**

That uncertainty is not a weakness in the story. It is where the story actually is.
