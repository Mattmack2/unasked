# Air-pollution filters can contain a second, unread measurement

A national air-quality network in the UK has already been used as a biodiversity sensor without changing what the stations were doing.

Researchers analyzed 185 samples from 15 sites in the Heavy Metals monitoring network. The filters had been collected to measure pollution. DNA recovered from them produced 1,227 taxa in the final eDNA dataset, spanning vertebrates, invertebrates, fungi, plants and protists.

That does not mean an air filter is a wildlife census. It means environmental infrastructure can collect more information than its designers intended.

The same study found 15 sequence variants assigned to organisms that do not occur in the UK. Some were consistent with laboratory contamination; others were likely taxonomic misassignments. Several groups were also systematically missed because DNA shedding, particle capture, PCR primers and reference databases differ by taxon. A 2026 moth study makes the limitation concrete: airborne eDNA found 41% fewer moth species than paired light traps, yet also found species the traps missed. Sequence counts did not track trap abundance.

So the useful system is not:

`air filter → biodiversity census`

It is closer to:

`air filter + molecular assay + atmospheric model + taxonomic validation → another biodiversity observation layer`

The retrospective version is more unusual.

A Swedish radionuclide-monitoring station had been changing and archiving air filters weekly for decades. Researchers sequenced 380 filters spanning 1974–2008 and recovered more than 2,700 genera. For a limited set of vertebrates, long-term eDNA patterns agreed with conventional monitoring well enough to support ecological trend reconstruction.

That result survived validation, but it should not be generalized casually. The Swedish station moved roughly 1,000 cubic metres of air per hour—more than 100,000 cubic metres per week—and stored filters individually in airtight containers. That is unusually favorable for recovering extremely dilute airborne DNA. I did not find an independent multi-decade archive reconstruction of comparable breadth.

Recent work also shows why storage details matter. Filter material and temperature can materially change how much DNA survives after capture. Existing archives therefore need to be tested, not assumed useful.

The broader opportunity is still large. Air-quality, radionuclide and other atmospheric networks already have fixed sites, power, maintenance routines, standardized sampling hardware and sometimes decades of stored material. Adding a molecular-analysis layer can be cheaper than building an entirely new ecological network, and old filters may contain observations from periods when conventional biodiversity surveys were sparse.

There is also a governance consequence. Shotgun sequencing of airborne eDNA can recover human genomic material as incidental bycatch. Standard biodiversity metabarcoding is not the same thing as identifying individuals, but a future network that routinely sequences atmospheric DNA at high depth would collect information about people as well as ecosystems.

The strongest conclusion is narrow:

**Some environmental-monitoring systems have been collecting a second scientific dataset by accident. New measurement methods can make that latent dataset readable years or decades later.**

That is more useful than calling airborne eDNA a replacement for cameras, traps or field surveys. Its value is that it sees different things, through infrastructure that often already exists.
