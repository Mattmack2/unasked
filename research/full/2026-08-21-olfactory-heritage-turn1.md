# 1. The simple version

Museums are very good at preserving things that can be put in a case, photographed, scanned or recorded.

Smell is awkward. It is volatile. The physical source changes as it ages. The same mixture can be perceived differently at different concentrations. People disagree about descriptors. Cultural meaning changes. A historic building can be conserved while the workshop, food market, incense practice, paper stock, fuel, animals or industrial processes that made its characteristic smell disappear.

There is now an interdisciplinary field called **olfactory heritage** that treats some smells as part of cultural heritage when they are meaningful to communities because of their connection to places, objects, practices or traditions.

The field does not generally solve the problem by trying to trap one sample of air forever.

Instead it can preserve several layers:

- the **material source** of the odour when possible;
- a chemical profile of emitted **volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**;
- which compounds actually matter to human perception;
- sensory descriptions from trained and untrained noses;
- historical records about what people smelled, valued, feared or associated with the scent;
- the practice or place that produced it;
- and, sometimes, a documented **reconstruction** that lets future people smell an approximation.

The important shift is therefore not “scientists can bottle history.” It is:

> **heritage conservation is beginning to treat an atmosphere as something that can be documented, interpreted and—in limited cases—reproduced.**

---

# 2. Why this is interesting

Most historical imagination is visually biased.

We know what a Victorian street looked like from photographs and paintings. We can hear old voices once sound recording appears. We can conserve a book, a factory, a church or a ritual object.

But many places and practices were recognized partly through smell. A tannery, old library, food market, railway, cocoa factory, church, pharmacy, working stable or ritual procession is not sensorially equivalent after its odours disappear.

The emerging field calls this inherited visual bias **ocularcentrism**. The claim is not that smell should replace sight. It is that what societies choose to document determines which dimensions of the past remain available later.

This gives the question a practical edge: sometimes we do not realize a smell carried information or identity until modernization, climate change, regulation, changed materials, new ventilation, altered land use or generational turnover removes it.

UNESCO's July 2026 feature on olfactory heritage makes this point explicitly: formal heritage practice rarely safeguards smell, even when a living tradition depends on aromatic materials or trained olfactory knowledge. The article uses Bahia's Lavagem do Bonfim, where aromatic waters and herbs form part of a long-lived ritual practice, as one example of sensory heritage that can change if plant availability or intergenerational practice changes.

Important caution: this UNESCO article is **not evidence that UNESCO has formally added smell as a protected heritage category**. Odeuropa's 2024 policy paper says almost the opposite: current World Heritage and Intangible Cultural Heritage frameworks do not specifically exclude sensory aspects, but olfactory heritage remains barely recognized in policy and nomination practice.

# 3. Vocabulary graft

## Olfactory heritage

Smells that have cultural significance because of their association with meaningful places, practices, objects or traditions.

## Smellscape

The olfactory dimension of a place as experienced and interpreted by people. A smellscape is not just a gas mixture; it includes the arrangement, salience and cultural meaning of odours in a setting.

## Keynote smell

A background smell that helps define a place but may barely be noticed until it disappears.

## Smell signal

A foreground odour that attracts conscious attention.

## Smellmark

A smell with distinctive significance to a community or place—the olfactory analogue of a landmark in the field's vocabulary.

## Odour / smell distinction

The field often distinguishes the material emission or stimulus (**odour**) from the human perceptual/cultural experience (**smell**). The same chemical source is not a complete description of the human experience.

## VOC — volatile organic compound

A carbon-containing compound that readily enters the gas phase. Many odorous materials emit complex VOC mixtures.

## Headspace sampling

Sampling the volatile molecules in the air immediately surrounding an object or material rather than destroying the object itself.

## HS-SPME

**Headspace solid-phase microextraction.** A coated fibre collects VOCs from the headspace and is then analysed instrumentally. It is useful for minimally invasive or non-destructive characterization of heritage objects.

## GC-MS

**Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry.** Separates volatile compounds and helps identify them chemically.

## GC-O / GC-MS/O

**Gas chromatography–olfactometry.** A human assessor smells separated compounds as they elute from a chromatograph, allowing researchers to distinguish chemicals that are present from chemicals that actually contribute strongly to perceived odour.

## Odour activity value (OAV)

A measure relating a compound's concentration to its sensory threshold. A trace compound can dominate perceived smell if humans are extremely sensitive to it.

## Sensomics

An approach that identifies the subset of chemical compounds that actually drive sensory perception rather than treating every detected molecule as equally important.

# 4. The key conceptual problem: a smell is not just a chemical fingerprint

A naive archive might work like this:

1. analyse the VOCs;
2. save the formula;
3. remake them later;
4. smell restored.

The literature says the problem is harder.

Chemical concentration and sensory perception are non-linear. Some molecules have very low odour thresholds; others can be abundant but contribute little to what a person notices. Mixtures interact. The background environment changes perception. People use different words for the same stimulus. Familiarity, expectation and cultural context matter.

This is why the 2026 historical-perfume study is important. The researchers did not stop at ordinary GC-MS. They combined chemical analysis with **olfactory detection**, calculated odour activity values, selected 13 compounds important to the perceived profile, performed omission tests, and compared the reconstruction with the historical perfume using a panel of 70 trained and untrained participants. They reported no statistically significant difference between the original sample and the reproduction under their test.

That is much stronger than “we found the molecules.”

It is still only one case, and the target was deliberately the **current smell of the aged perfume**, not a claim to recover what the perfume smelled like when first bottled in the early twentieth century.

That distinction may be one of the most important in the whole field.

---

# 5. Which past are you preserving?

A historical object's current scent is itself historical—but it may be very different from its original scent.

An old book's characteristic aroma emerges partly because cellulose, lignin, adhesives, leather, inks and other materials have aged and degraded. A 2009 Analytical Chemistry study showed that VOCs emitted by historic paper can carry information about paper composition and degradation state. Acetic acid, furfural and other degradation products contribute to the familiar odour and can even serve as non-destructive diagnostic signals for conservation.

So if a museum records the smell of a 200-year-old book in 2026, there are at least three different possible targets:

1. **the book's present aged smell**;
2. **the smell of the book when it was new**;
3. **the smellscape of the room or practice in which people historically encountered it**.

Those are not interchangeable.

A scientifically accurate reproduction of target 1 can still be historically wrong for target 2.

For lost practices, researchers may have no surviving source at all. They instead combine historical recipes, textual descriptions, images, material analysis, archaeology, ethnographic evidence and perfumery expertise. In those cases the output is better described as a **historically informed interpretation** than an authentic recovered smell.

Recent museum scholarship is unusually explicit about this. A 2025 article on recreating ancient Egyptian mummification scents argues that authenticity should be treated as a spectrum and that olfactory reproductions are interpretations informed by evidence, not perfect resurrection of the ancient sensory experience.

# 6. Old books are a useful test case because the smell carries two kinds of information

## A. Conservation information

The “old book smell” is partly the chemistry of material degradation.

The 2009 material-degradomics work correlated volatile degradation products with properties relevant to paper preservation, including lignin, rosin, cellulose polymerization and acidity. The smell is therefore not merely sentimental; emissions can contain diagnostic information about object condition.

## B. Cultural/perceptual information

Bembibre and Strlič's 2017 heritage-smell framework combined instrumental analysis with human descriptions.

Visitors to the historic library at St Paul's Cathedral commonly described its smell as woody, smoky, earthy and vanilla-like. In a separate test, 79 museum visitors smelled an extract based on a 1928 book without being told what it was; “chocolate/cocoa” was the most common descriptor, followed by coffee, old, wood and burnt.

The researchers used such results to create a **Historic Book Odour Wheel** linking sensory vocabulary to chemical characterization.

This is a useful example of why an olfactory archive needs both molecules and noses.

---

# 7. From one-off experiments to infrastructure

This is not only a handful of researchers making scented museum exhibits.

The EU-funded **Odeuropa** project (2021–2023) tried to build infrastructure for smell history and heritage. Its outputs include:

- an **Encyclopedia of Smell History and Heritage**;
- a Smell Explorer connecting historical text/image references;
- an **Olfactory Storytelling Toolkit** for museums;
- a semantic data model / European Olfactory Knowledge Graph;
- a **Heritage Smell Library**;
- and a 2024 policy toolkit aimed at helping institutions recognize and document olfactory heritage.

The Heritage Smell Library began with 19 documented scent creations, including frankincense, myrrh, a historical room, canal, cheese, civet, rosemary, tanned leather and other reconstructed or interpreted scents. The project explicitly treats documentation of cultural-historical significance as part of the scent object: a vial without provenance is not enough.

That is a subtle but important point.

The archive is not just a box of smells. It is a record of **what the smell represented, how it was reconstructed, what evidence supports it, and whose heritage value it carries**.

---

# 8. A current capability shift: reproducing a perceived smell, not merely detecting its chemistry

The June 2026 historical-perfume study is the strongest current methodological signal in this Turn 1.

Earlier heritage-smell work showed that researchers could sample VOCs, characterize old-paper odours, and build sensory vocabularies. The new work pushes toward a more demanding target: reconstruct a historical object's perceived current smell with enough chemical and sensory rigor that a panel cannot reliably distinguish it from the source under the experiment.

The pipeline was roughly:

```text
historic source
   ↓
GC-MS/O
   ↓
identify odour-active compounds
   ↓
rank via odour activity / sensory relevance
   ↓
formulate reconstruction
   ↓
omission tests + chemical validation
   ↓
trained + untrained sensory panel
```

This does not make smell preservation solved. It establishes a more disciplined method than “a perfumer smelled the object and made something evocative.”

A crucial boundary remains: a perfume flask is a relatively bounded olfactory source. Reproducing the smellscape of a market, church, industrial district or ritual procession is a much larger problem involving spatial mixing, changing activities, weather, seasonality, living organisms and cultural perception.

---

# 9. A second current frontier: reconstructing scents when only material traces survive

Ancient Egyptian embalming work provides a different route.

In 2023, researchers analysed labelled vessels from a Saqqara embalming workshop using organic residue chemistry and identified mixtures containing oils, tars, resins, fats, bitumen, beeswax and other materials associated with mummification. Separate work on 3,500-year-old mummification balms from Senetnay's canopic jars identified complex mixtures including beeswax, plant oils/fats, bitumen and aromatic resins.

Museum teams subsequently worked with perfumers to turn biomolecular findings into public scent reproductions such as the **Scent of the Afterlife**.

This is not direct capture of a surviving ancient smell. The modern recreation uses today's materials and safety constraints to interpret chemical evidence from ancient residues. The 2025 methodological discussion around the exhibition is explicit that such scents cannot be fully authentic.

But it changes what archaeological chemistry can do in public history: a residue analysis can become a sensory hypothesis a visitor can encounter directly.

That is a different kind of knowledge from reading “the mixture contained conifer resin.”

# 10. Why current heritage policy struggles with smell

Conservation institutions are built around categories such as tangible objects, buildings, landscapes and documented intangible practices.

Olfactory heritage cuts across them.

The smell may be:

- an attribute of an object;
- an attribute of a room or landscape;
- a product of a living practice;
- embodied expertise in the nose of a craftsperson;
- a chemical signal of material degradation;
- or a temporary interaction between people, weather, materials and activity.

Odeuropa's 2024 policy paper argues that current heritage conventions do not specifically exclude olfactory experience, but they rarely prompt communities or institutions to identify sensory dimensions explicitly. The result is a recognition gap rather than a clear legal ban.

This matters because some smell heritage cannot be recovered after disappearance. A recipe may survive while the plant variety changes. A market may survive while refrigeration and packaging remove its old smellscape. A traditional workshop may remain as architecture after the working materials and embodied practices disappear.

The policy question is therefore partly **when to document**, not just how to reconstruct later.

# 11. Strongest caveats

## 11.1 “Authentic smell” is often the wrong phrase

A reconstruction may target the present aged smell of an object, an inferred historical formula, or an interpretive evocation of a past practice. These need explicit labels.

## 11.2 Chemical equivalence is not perceptual equivalence

GC-MS can identify compounds without establishing how the mixture smells. Human olfaction, thresholds and interactions matter.

## 11.3 Perceptual similarity is not historical authenticity

A panel judging two smells similar does not establish that people centuries ago encountered the same smell under the same context, concentration or meaning.

## 11.4 Smellscapes are dynamic systems

A room or street is not a perfume bottle. Time of day, humidity, temperature, ventilation, activity and source mixture can change the profile.

## 11.5 Cultural meaning cannot be inferred from chemistry alone

The same odour may be sacred, disgusting, comforting, dangerous or mundane in different periods and communities.

## 11.6 Reconstruction can manipulate visitors

The 2017 framework notes curatorial concerns when synthetic scents are presented without clear provenance or when commercially designed aromas imply more certainty than evidence supports.

## 11.7 Safety and accessibility matter

Museum scenting can affect people with fragrance sensitivity, respiratory conditions, migraine triggers or other access needs. Indoor-air-quality constraints and consent are part of responsible deployment.

## 11.8 Not every old smell deserves preservation

Heritage value comes from significance, community meaning, knowledge or historical interpretation—not simply age or weirdness.

# 12. What this changes about the mental model of an archive

The useful synthesis from this Turn 1 is that smell forces a distinction that ordinary object conservation can hide:

> **an archive may preserve a source, a state, a recipe, a measurement, an experience, or a cultural meaning—and those are different preservation targets.**

For a painting, the physical object and the heritage object often feel like the same thing.

For smell they visibly separate.

A bottle containing a scent reconstruction is not the vanished ritual. A GC-MS chromatogram is not the smell. A written recipe is not the chemical state produced by historically specific ingredients. A sensory description is not the volatile mixture. A preserved room is not its old smellscape if the activities that generated it are gone.

Olfactory heritage therefore behaves less like “save the object” and more like **versioned preservation of a relationship between material source, human perception, historical context and cultural practice**.

This is a synthesis from the field rather than a claim that researchers use exactly this phrase.

Turn 2 should search for whether conservation theory already formalizes an equivalent temporal/target distinction before treating this as novel vocabulary.

# 13. Tangible present and future uses

## Already real

- **Conservation diagnostics:** VOC emissions from paper can provide information about material degradation without destructive sampling.
- **Historic-library documentation:** chemistry + sensory panels can record a place's characteristic odour vocabulary.
- **Museum interpretation:** reconstructed historical scents are used to communicate archaeological or historical material in multisensory exhibitions.
- **Heritage smell libraries:** documented reconstructions can be stored for research and reuse.
- **Living-practice documentation:** practitioners can record the sensory cues central to rituals or crafts before materials/practices change.

## Plausible next step

Institutions could begin creating **olfactory baselines** for threatened places and practices in the same way they create photographic, acoustic or environmental records:

```text
place/practice/object
  ↓
community significance + historical documentation
  ↓
season/time/context record
  ↓
VOC/headspace sample where appropriate
  ↓
sensory panel / practitioner vocabulary
  ↓
source materials / process record
  ↓
reconstruction protocol when justified
```

This would not guarantee resurrection. It would make later change visible and give future researchers more than a sentence saying “the old market had a distinctive smell.”

## Near-future possibility

A mature heritage standard might treat an olfactory reconstruction like a conservation model: versioned, evidence-linked and explicit about uncertainty. A future museum could let a visitor compare “current aged object,” “historically inferred original,” and “interpretive reconstruction” rather than presenting one scent as *the* authentic past.

That is a forecast, not current standard practice.

# 15. Conclusions that changed the answer

1. **Smell preservation is not primarily about trapping air.** The credible field combines chemical, sensory, historical and cultural records.
2. **The material odour and the human smell experience are not the same archival object.** This is why both instruments and noses are required.
3. **The target date matters.** Reproducing how an old object smells now can be scientifically rigorous while saying nothing about how it smelled when new.
4. **Current analytical methods are improving from compound detection toward perceptually validated reproduction.** The 2026 historical-perfume work is a real step here.
5. **Olfactory heritage is no longer only a conceptual argument.** It now has toolkits, an encyclopedia, a smell library, museum workflows and policy proposals.
6. **It is still institutionally peripheral.** Current policy architecture rarely asks communities to document sensory heritage explicitly.
7. **Some smells carry diagnostic information as well as cultural meaning.** Old-paper VOCs are a concrete example.
8. **Reconstruction is interpretation.** The more distant or vanished the source, the more important it is to expose the inference chain.
9. **The strongest future use may be baseline documentation before disappearance, not spectacular resurrection afterward.**
10. **Smell reveals a deeper archive problem:** preservation targets can be source, state, sensory experience, practice and meaning, and an institution must decide which one it is claiming to conserve.

---

# Provisional conclusion

**Yes, some smells can meaningfully be treated as cultural heritage—but “preserve the smell” is an underspecified instruction.**

The most defensible emerging practice is to preserve an evidence bundle: material source when available, volatile chemistry, perceptual characterization, cultural meaning, historical context, process/provenance and a reconstruction recipe when one can be responsibly justified.

The current frontier has moved beyond merely proving that old objects emit measurable VOCs. In a bounded 2026 perfume case, researchers reproduced the object's current smell using GC-MS/olfactometry, odour-activity analysis, omission tests and a 70-person sensory validation panel. Meanwhile, museums and heritage projects are starting to operationalize smell libraries, toolkits and reconstructed scent experiences.

The hard problem is no longer simply **can we measure a smell?**

It is:

> **Which version of a vanished sensory world are we claiming to preserve, and what evidence makes that reconstruction more than a persuasive perfume?**

**Do not publish this conclusion until Turn 2.**
