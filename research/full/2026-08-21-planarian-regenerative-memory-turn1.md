## Question

**If a planarian flatworm loses its entire brain and regenerates a new one, can a learned behavioral preference survive—and if so, what biological substrate could preserve enough information for the new brain to recover it?**

## 1. The simple biological setup

Planarians are freshwater flatworms with a centralized nervous system. Their head contains paired cephalic ganglia — a simple brain — connected to two ventral nerve cords that run along the body. They also possess abundant adult stem cells called neoblasts and extensive positional information in differentiated tissues. After major amputation, remaining tissue can rebuild missing organs, including a new brain.

A posterior fragment produced by cutting behind the head therefore presents a rare experiment:

`learn something`
`→ remove the cephalic ganglia`
`→ allow a new head and brain to regenerate`
`→ test whether prior training still changes later behavior`

Important correction: the posterior fragment is **brainless, not nerveless**. It retains ventral nerve cords, peripheral neurons, muscle, gut, stem cells and other tissues. Any surviving information could in principle persist in residual neural tissue rather than in an exotic non-neural memory store.

## 2. Why this question has such a strange scientific history

The modern question inherits a notorious 1950s–1970s controversy.

James McConnell and collaborators trained planarians in light/shock paradigms, cut trained worms, and reported that animals regenerated from tail fragments showed behavioral “savings” during retraining. McConnell then made the much stronger claim that learning could be transferred to naïve planarians by feeding them trained worms — the famous planarian cannibalism / “memory RNA” experiments.

Those claims generated a large literature and considerable publicity. They also generated replication failures, procedural disputes, weak controls and disagreement over whether planarians had been conditioned at all. The memory-transfer program ultimately lost scientific credibility. Later historical reviews distinguish two things that public retellings often collapse:

1. **Planarian learning and regenerative memory** were studied with multiple paradigms and had some positive replications.
2. **Transfer of a specific memory between animals via ingested RNA** was a much stronger claim that failed to become a reproducible foundation for memory science.

The colorful failure of the second claim helped bury the first question for decades.

## 3. The 2013 reboot was weaker — and better — than the headline version

Shomrat and Levin built an automated behavioral apparatus partly to avoid the subjective scoring and protocol drift that had plagued the older work.

They familiarized *Dugesia japonica* with a rough-bottomed environment containing food. Intact familiarized worms later approached food in that environment faster than unfamiliarized controls, and the difference persisted for at least 14 days.

Then they decapitated the worms far enough posteriorly that no brain tissue should remain and allowed new heads to regenerate.

The result needs careful wording.

When regenerated worms were tested directly, the previously familiarized group had a slightly shorter feeding latency, but the difference **was not statistically significant**.

The positive result appeared in a **savings paradigm**. Regenerated animals received one three-hour re-exposure/feeding session in the familiar environment four days before testing. After that refresher, worms descended from previously familiarized bodies reached the feeding criterion faster than regenerated controls that had not experienced the environment before decapitation (`P = 0.027` for latency; `P = 0.013` for the under-eight-minute criterion).

So the 2013 result is not clean evidence that a fully expressed memory simply waited outside the brain and appeared in a new one. It is evidence that **some latent consequence of earlier experience survived decapitation strongly enough to accelerate later re-expression or relearning after brain regeneration**.

The authors themselves considered several possibilities:

- a trace stored outside the brain and imprinted onto the new CNS;
- modification of the peripheral nervous system that later retrains the new brain unusually quickly;
- epigenetic or other state changes in somatic or stem-cell populations;
- another distributed physiological mechanism.

That is much more interesting than the oversimplified headline because it converts “where is the memory?” into a causal reconstruction problem.

Source:
- https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/216/20/3799/11714/An-automated-training-paradigm-reveals-long-term

## 4. A 2026 peer-reviewed experiment makes a stronger behavioral claim

Samuel, Hakes, Suviseshamuthu and Fichera published a 2026 *Biomolecules* experiment using sucrose-induced conditioned place preference rather than the 2013 environmental-familiarization task.

Untrained *Girardia* preferred a rough surface. Fifteen worms were repeatedly exposed to their initially unpreferred smooth surface paired with 10% sucrose. After training, the group shifted strongly toward the smooth surface. A D1 dopamine-receptor antagonist prevented the corresponding conditioned preference in another cohort, providing a pharmacological manipulation consistent with prior planarian reward work.

The critical regeneration experiment was small: **six sucrose-trained animals** and six sucrose-plus-antagonist animals were each divided into anterior and posterior segments and allowed to regenerate for seven days.

Both head-derived and tail-derived regenerates from sucrose-trained worms spent more time on the previously conditioned smooth surface. In the tail-derived comparison, the BH-corrected post-hoc result was `p = 0.0480`, with a reported large effect size (`d = 1.31`). The head-derived result was `p = 0.0458`.

The antagonist-trained regenerates largely retained the innate rough-surface preference instead.

This is stronger than the 2013 savings result because the learned preference was reported after regeneration **without a special retraining session**.

But Turn 2 needs to be hard on it:

- the post-regeneration group is only `n = 6` original animals;
- the key corrected p-values sit just below 0.05;
- the authors correctly acknowledge that behavior cannot distinguish peripheral neural storage, epigenetic state, RNA, neoblast-associated mechanisms or regeneration-dependent reconstruction;
- immediate post-amputation behavior can be contaminated by injury physiology;
- the paradigm measures a persistent preference state, which may not map cleanly onto what neuroscientists usually mean by a specific declarative or associative memory.

The paper itself labels the substrate unresolved.

Sources:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13204754/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42194000/

## 5. The most important 2026 counterevidence attacks the first step: can these worms be conditioned reliably at all?

A 2026 bioRxiv preprint from Zachary Kelso, Madeleine Snyder and Samuel Gershman tried to reproduce classical planarian conditioning with modern controls.

They recreated the old light/shock protocol, used paired versus pseudoconditioned controls, tested multiple sensory combinations, tested two species and multiple geographic lineages, and built a computer-vision pipeline so the result did not depend entirely on human observers.

They found **no compelling evidence for classical conditioning in any tested paradigm**.

In their Jacobson-style replication, conditioned responses decreased rather than increased across days, and the paired and pseudoconditioned groups did not significantly differ. They also attempted to reproduce a more recent fear-conditioning-style protocol and again found no acquisition by manual scoring or computer vision.

Their concern about historical measurement is concrete: manual scorers often disagreed substantially about what counted as the conditioned contraction. They argue that a weakly reproducible learning assay cannot support a strong claim about memory surviving brain regeneration.

This preprint does **not directly refute** the 2013 familiarization paradigm or the 2026 sucrose conditioned-place-preference result; those are different tasks. But it changes the burden of proof. “Planarians can learn” cannot be treated as one interchangeable capability across paradigms.

Source:
- https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.18.719399v2.full

## 6. The field is currently producing contradictory evidence in real time

A second 2026 bioRxiv preprint, posted August 11 by Dev, Nguyen and Levin, again reports memory through head regeneration using another paradigm.

The worms were trained to override normal light avoidance by associating light with food, decapitated, allowed roughly two weeks to regenerate, and then retested. The authors report that a majority of regenerated animals retained the learned response.

This is currently **preprint evidence**, not a peer-reviewed confirmation. It comes from the Levin group, which has long argued for distributed biological information processing, so independent replication will matter especially strongly.

The result also lands only months after the Gershman-group preprint failed across several classical-conditioning variants.

The correct Turn 1 state is therefore not:

`scientists proved memories survive decapitation`

It is:

`multiple paradigms report experience-dependent behavior surviving brain regeneration`
`+ a modern replication effort cannot reproduce foundational conditioning`
`+ the biological storage/reconstruction mechanism is unknown`
`→ an unusually clean live replication frontier`

## 7. “Memory outside the brain” contains several very different hypotheses

If the positive behavioral results survive independent replication, at least five mechanisms remain plausible enough to separate experimentally.

### A. Residual nervous-system memory

Removing the cephalic ganglia does not remove the ventral nerve cords or peripheral nervous system. Training could alter those neural circuits. During regeneration, the new brain could reconnect to an already modified network.

This is the least exotic explanation and therefore an essential control.

### B. Peripheral state that makes relearning faster

The body might not preserve a representation of “rough surface means food.” It might preserve altered receptor sensitivity, motor bias, motivational state or another physiological change that makes reacquisition much faster.

This possibility fits the 2013 result particularly well because direct post-regeneration recall was not significant but a single refresher revealed savings.

### C. Molecular / epigenetic state in non-neural tissue

Training could leave stable transcriptional, chromatin, RNA or biochemical changes in tissues that survive the original brain. During regeneration, those states might bias the construction or tuning of the new nervous system.

This remains a mechanism class, not a demonstrated encoding scheme.

### D. Stem-cell-mediated reconstruction

Neoblasts generate new tissue during regeneration. An experience-dependent signal might alter neoblast state or the environment instructing them, allowing a regenerated brain to be built differently.

Again, there is no direct evidence yet that neoblasts encode the tested behavioral associations.

### E. Distributed bioelectric state

Planarian tissues use gap junctions, ion channels and membrane-voltage patterns to regulate anatomical regeneration. Transient physiological perturbations can alter the morphology later regenerated by descendants of treated fragments without changing DNA sequence.

This establishes that distributed physiological state can carry **anatomical pattern information** through regeneration.

It does **not** establish that learned behavioral memory uses the same substrate. “Pattern memory” in regenerative biology and “memory of an experienced environment” are currently different phenomena that share suggestive vocabulary.

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6234102/
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7706840/

## 8. The anatomical-memory connection is useful precisely because it is not yet the answer

Planarians already provide a striking example of information surviving cellular turnover and injury: each fragment knows enough about body axes and missing structures to rebuild a proportioned organism.

The underlying biology is increasingly concrete. Position-control genes expressed in differentiated tissues encode regional identity; Wnt signaling contributes to anterior/posterior decisions; muscle contains positional information; neoblasts supply new cells. Separate work shows that bioelectric and gap-junction perturbations can stably change the morphology a fragment regenerates.

That makes a broader question unavoidable:

**How many different kinds of information can a body preserve outside a particular organ?**

But it would be a mistake to jump from anatomical patterning to autobiographical memory. A body plan is a highly conserved developmental target. A learned association is contingent information acquired during one animal's life. The evidentiary bar for showing that the same information architecture stores both should be high.

## 9. Why the current moment is better than the 1960s

The question used to be constrained by subjective behavior scoring and almost no access to the molecular state of the animal.

A serious modern program can combine:

- automated tracking and preregistered behavioral criteria;
- blinded paired/pseudoconditioned controls;
- multiple independent learning paradigms;
- lineage-resolved single-cell transcriptomics;
- spatial transcriptomics;
- targeted neuronal ablation beyond simple decapitation;
- manipulation of ventral nerve cords and peripheral neural pathways;
- RNA sequencing and epigenomic profiling before and after training;
- neoblast depletion or transplantation;
- bioelectric and gap-junction interventions;
- repeated regeneration cycles;
- open video/data release.

The frontier has therefore changed from “can someone make this weird behavior happen?” to “can we localize the minimal state variable that carries the behavioral difference across reconstruction?”

That is a much more falsifiable question.

## 10. The research trajectory that matters

The first attractive version was simple:

**A worm can remember after you cut off its brain.**

The evidence immediately forced four corrections.

1. **“Brainless” is not “without nervous tissue.”** Posterior fragments retain ventral nerve cords and peripheral neurons.
2. **The best-known modern positive result from 2013 was savings, not clean direct recall.** The difference emerged after one brief re-exposure to the old environment.
3. **A 2026 peer-reviewed sucrose study reports direct post-regeneration preference, but its decisive regeneration cohort is small (`n = 6`) and the corrected significance is marginal.**
4. **A separate 2026 group could not reliably demonstrate classical conditioning across multiple protocols at all.** The positive phenomenon therefore cannot inherit confidence from the old literature as a whole.

Then a fifth fact made the topic more interesting rather than less: a new August 2026 preprint again reports retained learned behavior after regeneration using a different task.

The best current question is no longer “where does the worm store a memory?”

It is:

> **What is the smallest experience-dependent state that can survive destruction of the original brain and measurably bias the behavior of the regenerated animal?**

That wording permits several outcomes: a true distributed memory trace, residual peripheral neural plasticity, a physiological bias that accelerates relearning, or failure of the behavioral phenomenon under independent replication.

## 11. Tangible implications if the phenomenon survives

The immediate importance is foundational neuroscience, not a direct human therapy claim.

### Memory storage

Synaptic plasticity in the brain clearly participates in ordinary memory. A reproducible planarian case would show that at least some learned-state information can also be stabilized in a substrate that survives removal of the original cephalic ganglia.

That would not mean human memories are stored in muscle or that brains are unnecessary.

### Regenerative neuroscience

As neural-replacement therapies improve, a real biological example of a nervous system reconstructing function under influence from surviving tissue would become relevant to questions about how newly generated neurons integrate into old behavioral states.

The current planarian evidence is far too distant from humans to promise memory preservation after human brain repair.

### Information inheritance within one body

Regeneration forces an unusual distinction between **material continuity** and **informational continuity**. Most of the original brain can be gone while the organism remains causally continuous through other tissues. Planaria make it experimentally possible to ask which acquired states travel across that transition.

### Better experimental decomposition of “memory”

The disagreement itself may be productive. Classical conditioning, place preference, environmental familiarization, reward state and light-food association may rely on different neural and bodily substrates. Rather than asking whether “memory” survives, researchers can ask which components survive which kinds of reconstruction.

## 13. Provisional conclusion

There is credible modern evidence that **some consequences of prior learning can survive planarian head removal and influence behavior after a new brain regenerates**. The 2013 automated study found latent savings after a refresher. A 2026 peer-reviewed sucrose study reports conditioned place preference after regeneration from tail fragments. An August 2026 preprint reports a similar broad phenomenon with light-food training.

But this is not a settled fact about memory storage. A separate 2026 replication effort failed to establish classical conditioning across several planarian protocols, and the strongest direct peer-reviewed regeneration result uses only six original animals in its critical cohort.

The most defensible frontier is therefore mechanistic and adversarial:

**If an independently reproducible behavioral trace crosses brain regeneration, determine the minimal surviving substrate before calling it “memory outside the brain.”**

## Turn 2 attack plan

1. Independently audit the 2026 *Biomolecules* statistics, design, pseudo-replication risk and controls, especially the `n=6` regeneration cohort.
2. Determine whether the reward CPP paradigm has independent replication in planarians and whether surface preference can persist through non-associative mechanisms.
3. Compare the Gershman null preprint's paradigms directly with the positive 2013, 2026 peer-reviewed and August 2026 preprint tasks; identify which disagreements are genuine replication conflicts versus different forms of learning.
4. Inspect the August 2026 Dev/Nguyen/Levin preprint's sample sizes, controls, blinding and effect sizes.
5. Search for independent post-2013 replications of memory-through-head-regeneration outside the Levin lineage.
6. Clarify exactly which nervous structures remain in each positive experiment's posterior fragment and whether peripheral neural storage has ever been ablated or excluded.
7. Look for neoblast-depletion, irradiation, transplantation, RNA, epigenetic or bioelectric experiments that directly couple an acquired behavioral state to a surviving substrate.
8. Separate “anatomical pattern memory” from learned behavioral memory; challenge any literature that slides between the two without mechanism.
9. Search for alternative explanations: altered metabolism, sucrose sensitization, innate surface preference, injury response, peripheral receptor changes, microbiome, slime trails, residual environmental cues and experimenter effects.
10. Decide whether the strongest public piece should be a positive finding or a live scientific conflict: **a 70-year-old claim has finally become experimentally tractable, and modern labs still disagree about the first premise.**
