## Validated answer

There is credible modern evidence that prior experience can leave a behavioral effect detectable after a planarian loses its original head and regenerates a new brain. The strongest current evidence does **not** justify saying that a specific memory has been proven to reside in non-neural body tissue or that the regenerated brain somehow reconstructs an intact engram from the body.

The better conclusion is narrower:

> **Some training-induced state may survive removal of the original cephalic ganglia and later bias behavior after regeneration, but both the behavioral phenomenon and its substrate remain unsettled.**

Three evidence streams matter.

1. Shomrat & Levin (2013) found that familiarized worms did not show significant direct recall after head regeneration, but did show faster performance after one re-exposure session — a `savings` effect.
2. Samuel et al. (2026, peer reviewed) found a sucrose-associated surface preference after regeneration from posterior fragments, but the decisive regeneration cohort contained only six trained worms and lacked a clean unpaired-reward control.
3. Kelso, Snyder & Gershman (2026 preprint) failed to reproduce classical conditioning across multiple protocols, species/lineages, stimulus combinations and both manual and computer-vision scoring. Their null results do not directly test the sucrose place-preference assay, but they establish that planarian learning assays are much less robust than the historical literature implies.

An August 2026 Levin-group preprint reports a separate light-food conditioning result surviving two weeks of head regeneration. It is recent, not peer reviewed, and does not resolve the contradiction.

The central mechanistic correction is anatomical: a posterior fragment is **brainless but not nerveless**. Removing the cephalic ganglia leaves the paired ventral nerve cords and peripheral sensory/neural structures in the body. A positive regeneration result therefore does not by itself imply memory in muscle, stem cells, RNA, bioelectric fields or other non-neural tissue. Persistent plasticity in the surviving nervous system is already a serious candidate.

## Claim ledger

### 1. Planarians regenerate a new brain after decapitation

**Status: established.**

The planarian CNS consists of paired cephalic ganglia connected to two longitudinal ventral nerve cords. Head removal behind the cephalic ganglia eliminates the original brain, while posterior tissue retains the nerve cords and other body systems. Regeneration rebuilds the missing anterior CNS.

This supports the phrase `new brain`, but not `entirely new nervous system`.

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959437X15000106
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23821717/

### 2. The 2013 result showed intact memory immediately returning in the regenerated brain

**Status: rejected.**

Shomrat & Levin used environmental familiarization with a textured feeding arena. Intact worms retained the effect for at least 14 days. After decapitation and head regeneration, previously familiarized worms initially had slightly shorter feeding latency than controls, but **the difference was not statistically significant**.

The positive regeneration result appeared in a `savings` paradigm. Regenerated familiarized and unfamiliarized worms received one three-hour exposure/feeding session in the familiarization environment. Four days later, the previously familiarized group performed significantly faster (one-tailed U-test p=0.027; criterion-count Fisher test p=0.013).

That is evidence that previous experience may have left a latent trace that changed relearning. It is not direct recall of an intact learned behavior from a new brain.

Source:
- https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.087809

### 3. A 2026 peer-reviewed experiment found a learned preference after regeneration from posterior fragments

**Status: survives, but much weaker than the abstract suggests.**

Samuel et al. trained Girardia planarians by repeatedly pairing their initially non-preferred smooth surface with 10% sucrose while the preferred rough surface was paired with water. Fifteen worms reversed their baseline texture preference after training.

For the decisive post-regeneration experiment, however, the authors used a separate cohort of only **six sucrose-trained worms**. Each was split into anterior and posterior pieces, allowed to regenerate for seven days, and tested on a half-rough/half-smooth dish. Both head-derived and tail-derived regenerated animals spent more time on the smooth surface. The BH-adjusted post-hoc values were p=0.0458 for head-derived animals and p=0.0480 for tail-derived animals, with large reported effect sizes.

This is a positive result and deserves replication. It is not yet a high-certainty demonstration because the sample is tiny and the decisive tail comparison sits just inside conventional significance after correction.

Source:
- https://www.mdpi.com/2218-273X/16/5/649

### 4. The 2026 sucrose experiment proves associative memory rather than another training-induced preference change

**Status: not established.**

The design compares baseline texture preference before training with preference after repeated exposure in which sucrose is always associated with the smooth surface. It includes untrained animals for immediate post-amputation comparisons and a dopamine-antagonist cohort, but the post-regeneration stage does not include a conventional `unpaired` or `yoked` reward control in which animals receive the same sucrose exposure without a predictive surface association.

That leaves alternatives such as sensitization, altered surface valuation, persistent chemosensory or mechanosensory changes, or other non-associative training effects less cleanly separated than they would be in a rigorous associative-learning design.

The D1-antagonist result shows that dopamine receptor blockade during training prevents the preference shift under those conditions. It does **not** locate the stored information or prove that dopamine-dependent memory is distributed across body tissues.

Source:
- https://www.mdpi.com/2218-273X/16/5/649

### 5. A major 2026 replication failure means planarians cannot learn

**Status: rejected as too broad; the null result is still highly consequential.**

Kelso, Snyder & Gershman attempted to reproduce classical planarian conditioning with paired versus pseudoconditioned controls. They tested the historical light-shock paradigm, several alternative sensory pairings, two species and several Girardia populations. They also attempted a newer fear-conditioning-style protocol with 48 conditioned and 12 pseudoconditioned worms and built a computer-vision analysis pipeline.

They found no convincing acquisition of a conditioned response. In their historical-style scoring experiment, responses actually declined over days and paired animals did not differ significantly from pseudoconditioned controls. Manual event annotation also showed poor agreement among scorers; unanimous agreement was 28.15% for contraction events and 15.14% for turns in the tested footage.

This does not directly refute sucrose-conditioned place preference, environmental familiarization, operant learning or every other planarian assay. It does show that the foundational classical-conditioning literature is not a secure platform from which to make extraordinary claims about memory transfer or non-neural storage.

Source:
- https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.18.719399v2.full

### 6. The new August 2026 experiment resolves the dispute

**Status: no.**

Dev, Nguyen & Levin report in an August 11 bioRxiv preprint that planarians trained to override photophobia in a light-food paradigm retained the learned response after decapitation and two weeks of regeneration. The result is directionally important because it uses a different task from the sucrose texture study.

But it is a preprint posted only days before this Turn 2 and has not yet undergone peer review or independent replication. It is evidence to watch, not a verdict.

Source:
- https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.10.741213v1

### 7. If the effect is real, the memory must be stored outside the nervous system

**Status: rejected.**

Posterior fragments retain the ventral nerve cords and peripheral neural/sensory structures. Shomrat & Levin explicitly listed several possibilities in 2013, including memory execution or persistent modification in the peripheral nervous system, as well as the possibility that a changed PNS rapidly retrains a newly regenerated brain.

Non-neural candidates — altered gene expression, epigenetic state, RNA, stem-cell state, bioelectric physiological networks — remain interesting hypotheses, but current behavioral experiments do not discriminate among them.

Sources:
- https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.087809
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959437X15000106
- https://www.mdpi.com/2218-273X/16/5/649

### 8. RNA memory or cannibalistic memory transfer is an established explanation

**Status: rejected.**

The famous 1950s–60s claims that trained-planarian memory could be transferred by cannibalism or RNA became historically influential and experimentally controversial. Modern regeneration results do not validate those claims. A later UCL doctoral program that developed a rapid associative assay reported evidence for post-regeneration behavioral retention but did **not** find support for RNA as the substrate in its targeted experiments.

The current problem should therefore be studied from the phenomenon upward rather than selecting an exotic storage mechanism in advance.

Source:
- https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10097948/

## Strongest negative result

The Harvard/Gershman study changes the burden of proof. It did not merely fail once. It used multiple stimulus combinations, multiple worm sources, pseudoconditioned controls and automated scoring, yet failed to reproduce robust classical conditioning. It also found that human observers often disagree about the behavioral events that historical papers treated as conditioned responses.

This means a future regenerative-memory experiment should first earn a reliable learning effect **before amputation**, with preregistered outcome definitions and appropriate unpaired controls. Only then should a post-regeneration comparison be interpreted as memory persistence.

## What Turn 2 changed

Turn 1 framed the puzzle as `where does memory go while the brain is gone?`

Turn 2 shows that this asks for a storage mechanism too early.

The first unresolved layer is behavioral: which planarian learning paradigms reproducibly create an experience-dependent state that survives regeneration? The second is anatomical: does that state require surviving peripheral neural structures, or can it survive after those are disrupted too? Only after those are established does it make sense to ask whether epigenetics, RNA, stem cells, bioelectric state or another non-neural substrate stores information.

The best current question is therefore:

> **What is the smallest experience-dependent state that survives removal of the original brain, and which surviving tissue is necessary for that state to bias the regenerated animal?**

That formulation turns a sensational memory claim into an experimentally tractable causal map.

## Verdict

`SURVIVES_WITH_MAJOR_CORRECTIONS`.

The public story should not be **“worms remember after their brains are removed.”** The evidence is not mature enough.

The stronger and more durable story is:

**Planarians give researchers a rare way to ask how little of an old nervous system must survive for past experience to bias a newly regenerated brain — and right now the field cannot yet agree on the behavioral assay needed to answer it.**
