## Research question

**Are ancient tooth-enamel proteins becoming a practical molecular archive for periods where recoverable DNA is usually gone, and what new historical/evolutionary questions does that make answerable?**

---

## The simple version

DNA is the richest molecular record of an organism, but it is chemically fragile. Go far enough back in time—especially in warm environments—and useful DNA usually disappears.

Proteins are poorer records than whole genomes, but some survive longer. Tooth enamel is unusually dense and can trap a small set of proteins laid down while the tooth formed. Modern mass spectrometry can break the surviving fragments into measurable ions and infer parts of their amino-acid sequences.

That means some fossils once limited to **shape, location, and geological context** can now yield a small amount of molecular information too.

This is not Jurassic Park. It is closer to recovering a few durable sentences after the original library has burned.

---

## Why this is interesting

Palaeontology and human-evolution research have a large temporal gap between two kinds of evidence:

- morphology can persist for millions of years but can be ambiguous;
- ancient DNA can be extraordinarily informative but normally survives for a much shorter period and is strongly limited by environment.

Palaeoproteomics partially occupies the space between them.

The important shift is not merely “scientists found proteins in an old tooth.” It is:

> **some parts of the fossil record that were molecularly silent are becoming molecularly queryable.**

That changes what kind of question can be asked of an old specimen. Instead of only “what does this fossil look like?”, researchers may sometimes ask about evolutionary relationships, biological sex markers, species identity, or specific amino-acid variants.

---

## Where it becomes tangible

This is already being used, not just proposed.

### Molecular information from *Homo erectus*

A 2026 Nature study analysed enamel proteins from six Middle Pleistocene *Homo erectus* specimens, roughly 400,000 years old, from three sites in China. The researchers reported sex assignments from amelogenin evidence and amino-acid variants shared across specimens. One variant had previously been observed in Denisovan material, leading the authors to discuss possible evolutionary interaction or introgression scenarios.

That does **not** equal a reconstructed *H. erectus* genome, and the Denisovan connection is an inference from limited protein variants, not genome-wide proof. But molecular comparison in these specimens was previously extremely difficult.

### Investigating an unusual *Homo naledi* assemblage

A 2026 Cell study used micro-destructive acid etching on enamel from 23 *H. naledi* specimens representing at least 20 individuals. It found no convincing male markers in the analysed set and recovered two notable amino-acid substitutions. This demonstrates a relatively sustainable way to extract molecular clues from precious hominin teeth.

The result should not be simplified to “all *H. naledi* in the cave were female.” The paper itself frames the absence of confident male identification more carefully, and sampling/biological interpretation remain separate from analytical detection.

### Going dramatically deeper in non-human fossils

A 2025 Nature study recovered identifiable fragments of several enamel proteins from East African rhinocerotid and proboscidean fossils around 16–18 million years old, even in the warm Turkana Basin. The sequences were sparse, and the authors explicitly warned about fragment-identification uncertainty and possible sequence alteration during fossilization.

This does **not** mean we now have 18-million-year-old mammalian genomes. It shows that useful fragments of protein sequence can persist far deeper than the normal ancient-DNA window.

## 1. Vocabulary graft

### Palaeoproteomics / paleoproteomics
The study of ancient proteins preserved in archaeological and palaeontological materials.

### Proteome
The set of proteins present in a biological system. Ancient “proteomes” are normally extremely incomplete remnants of the original proteome.

### Peptide
A short chain of amino acids. Ancient proteins break into peptide fragments; mass spectrometry often works by identifying these fragments.

### Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS)
A method that measures peptide ions, fragments selected ions again, and uses the resulting spectra to infer peptide sequence/identity.

### Enamel proteome
A small protein set associated with tooth-enamel formation, including proteins such as amelogenin, enamelin and ameloblastin. Mature enamel is highly mineralized, making it an unusually durable molecular substrate.

### Diagenesis
Chemical/physical changes that occur after burial. Ancient peptides can fragment and chemically modify over geological time; those changes can destroy information but can also provide evidence relevant to authenticity.

### ZooMS
Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry, typically collagen peptide mass fingerprinting used to identify taxonomic groups in fragmentary bone. It is a related high-throughput palaeoproteomic tool, though different from deep sequence reconstruction from enamel.

### Phylogenetic inference
Using inherited molecular differences to infer evolutionary relationships among organisms.

## 2. Genealogy: this is an old dream with a new instrument stack

The idea of extracting biological information from ancient proteins predates modern DNA research. Ancient-protein investigations reach back to the 1930s and “paleobiochemistry” work in the 1950s.

Early approaches were constrained by a brutal problem: old proteins are degraded, chemically altered, mixed with environmental material, and easily contaminated. Immunological techniques could produce apparent detections without revealing enough sequence information to confidently distinguish ancient endogenous signal from cross-reactivity or contamination.

The major enabler was not an LLM. It was the development of modern **soft-ionization mass spectrometry**, high-resolution tandem MS, ancient-biomolecule laboratory practice, sequence databases, and computational methods able to work with highly fragmented/modified peptides.

So the accurate frontier statement is:

> Ancient-protein research is not newly invented. Its information yield and reachable time depth have materially expanded as measurement, authentication and computation improved.

---

## 3. Why enamel matters

Protein survival is substrate-dependent. Tooth enamel is the hardest tissue in the vertebrate body and is overwhelmingly mineral. Some proteins involved in enamel formation become trapped in that mineral structure.

This gives enamel several attractive properties:

- better molecular preservation than many softer tissues;
- resistance to some environmental degradation;
- a small/simple enough protein repertoire to make ancient fragments tractable;
- potential sex information through amelogenin X/Y peptides in some cases;
- phylogenetically informative amino-acid substitutions.

It also creates a limitation: enamel contains far less molecular information than a genome and a much narrower protein repertoire than many soft tissues.

---

## 4. Capability discontinuity

### Old bottleneck

For fossils beyond useful aDNA preservation, molecular phylogeny and individual-level biological information were often impossible or extremely sparse.

### New/current enablers

- high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry;
- sample-preparation protocols designed for degraded enamel peptides;
- authentication based on damage/fragmentation context and controls;
- de novo / error-tolerant sequence interpretation;
- larger comparative protein/genome databases;
- improved phylogenetic methods;
- micro-destructive sampling that can reduce damage to rare specimens.

### Enabler-removal test

Remove modern mass spectrometry and associated computational/authentication methods. The field does not merely become slower; much of the molecular information becomes inaccessible.

This therefore looks like a genuine **measurement capability shift**, not a rebranding of an old manual practice.

---

## 5. How far can it go?

There is no single “protein survival limit.” Preservation depends on tissue, temperature, burial chemistry, fossilization history, protein type, and what level of sequence information is required.

A 2024 Nature Protocols workflow describes useful enamel sequence recovery around the million-year scale and notes that in temperate/subtropical regions ancient proteins can survive well beyond the known limits of aDNA in comparable conditions.

The 2025 Turkana work pushes identifiable enamel fragments into the Early Miocene at roughly 18 Ma in a persistently warm region. That is remarkable, but the sequences become sparse with age and preservation differs sharply by site.

The correct mental model is therefore not:

> proteins survive X times longer than DNA.

It is:

> **different molecular archives fail at different rates and preserve different kinds of information. Enamel has opened a much deeper but lower-bandwidth channel.**

---

## 6. What can researchers actually learn?

### Taxonomy from otherwise uninformative fragments

Protein fingerprints can identify species/groups from bone fragments whose morphology is too incomplete to classify confidently. That can expand the usable archaeological record rather than merely adding detail to spectacular museum specimens.

### Evolutionary relationships

Sequence differences in ancient proteins can contribute to phylogenetic placement where DNA cannot be recovered.

### Sex estimation

Sex-linked enamel proteins can sometimes provide molecular sex information from teeth, though missing markers and preservation/technical limits must be interpreted carefully.

### Diet and cultural practice

Other palaeoproteomic substrates—dental calculus, pottery residues, food crusts—can retain food and activity-related proteins. This is a broader application of the field rather than an enamel-specific claim.

### Past disease and ecology

Ancient proteomics can contribute evidence about pathogens, human–animal interactions, and environments, again depending heavily on substrate and authentication.

---

## 7. A subtle future advantage: old data may become more informative without resampling the fossil

Mass-spectrometry datasets are digital artifacts. The 2022 field review emphasizes that protein identification depends strongly on sequence databases, search algorithms, assumed modifications, and computing methods.

That means some identification limitations are **soft limits**. A spectrum that could not be confidently assigned today may become interpretable later if:

- a related species is sequenced;
- protein databases improve;
- de novo sequencing improves;
- search models improve;
- better chemical-damage models become available.

This creates a useful archival principle:

> **preserving raw spectra may preserve future scientific optionality, even when the physical specimen cannot be sampled again.**

That is an inference from current methodology, not yet evidence that every old dataset will gain new discoveries.

---

## 8. Where machine learning could matter—and where the story gets dangerous

Ancient proteomics contains several computational bottlenecks that are plausible ML targets:

- spectrum interpretation;
- de novo peptide sequencing;
- retention-time / fragmentation prediction;
- distinguishing plausible ancient modifications from artifacts;
- protein/database search;
- triaging unidentified spectra;
- integrating sparse sequence evidence with phylogeny.

But this is an especially dangerous domain for “AI found an ancient protein” storytelling. The field already struggles with contamination, incomplete databases, uncertain sequence modifications and search-space choices. A more flexible model can create more candidate interpretations without making them true.

The right architecture is therefore familiar:

> **generation gets cheaper; authentication must remain rigid.**

---

## 9. The strongest caveats

### Proteins are not genomes

A few protein sequences contain vastly less hereditary information than whole-genome DNA. Protein evidence can constrain relationships without resolving them cleanly.

### Detection is biased

Some proteins and peptides survive, extract, ionize, and fragment better than others. Failure to detect a protein is often not evidence that it was absent in life.

### Databases bias identification

Well-studied species have vastly richer reference proteomes than poorly studied taxa. Search algorithms can return misleading high-scoring matches if the database is incomplete or structurally biased.

### Contamination is central, not incidental

Modern human proteins, lab reagents, microbes, conservation materials, handling, and burial environments can all introduce confusing signal. Ancient biomolecule work requires controls and authentication.

### Very old sequences become sparse

The 18 Ma result is not a complete proteome. The Nature paper explicitly reports fragment decline with age and warns about sparse fragments, identification uncertainty and possible sequence diagenesis.

### Molecular evidence does not erase archaeological interpretation

A sex-associated peptide, amino-acid variant or phylogenetic placement does not answer social, behavioral, depositional or cultural questions by itself.

## 10. What the near future could look like

This is a bounded forecast, not an established result.

If sensitivity, micro-sampling, databases and computational interpretation continue improving, a plausible next phase is **molecular triage of collections previously considered beyond the DNA frontier**.

That could look like:

1. minimally sample many teeth/fragments;
2. rapidly identify which preserve useful endogenous protein;
3. use peptide fingerprints for taxonomic screening;
4. reserve deeper MS/MS analysis for high-value specimens;
5. compare variants across time and geography;
6. re-analyse stored spectra as reference databases improve;
7. integrate protein evidence with morphology, chronology, isotopes and—where available—DNA.

The transformative possibility is not one spectacular fossil. It is changing the **denominator**: more of the world's existing fossil collections become candidates for molecular questions.

Whether that happens at large scale depends on cost, throughput, museum sampling ethics, data standards, preservation rates, and how much biological information the surviving proteins actually carry.

## 11. Candidate new synthesis from this Turn 1

### Hypothesis

The most consequential palaeoproteomics frontier may be less “older record achieved” and more **persistent molecular optionality**:

> minimally destructive sampling + raw spectral archives + improving reference databases may let old fossils and even old measurements gain new interpretability over time.

### Status

`DISCOVERY CANDIDATE` if this angle is promoted later. It is a synthesis/inference from the field's current measurement and database properties, not a newly demonstrated empirical effect.

### What would validate it

- documented cases where previously collected ancient-protein spectra produced materially new identifications after database/software advances;
- repositories preserving suitable raw data at scale;
- evidence that reanalysis yields scientifically useful changes rather than mostly marginal additional matches.

Turn 2 should specifically search for such cases before using this angle in a published article.

## Provisional conclusion

**Yes, with important bandwidth limits.** Dental-enamel palaeoproteomics appears to have crossed from an exotic proof-of-concept into a real molecular method for otherwise inaccessible time depths. Current work can recover evolutionary and individual-level clues from some fossils where DNA is unavailable, and 2025–2026 results show the accessible time horizon is still moving.

The field is not replacing ancient DNA. It is creating a lower-bandwidth molecular archive beneath it.

The strongest Unasked story is therefore not “scientists read impossibly old proteins.” It is:

> **The human past has multiple molecular archives. We built our historical imagination around the one called DNA; another archive is now making older parts of the fossil record partially queryable.**

**Do not publish this conclusion until Turn 2.**
