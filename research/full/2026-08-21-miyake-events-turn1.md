## 1. What a Miyake event is

Atmospheric radiocarbon (^14C) is continuously produced when energetic particles generate neutrons that interact with nitrogen in the atmosphere. The resulting carbon enters atmospheric CO₂, is taken up by plants and becomes preserved in annual tree rings.

Ordinary solar and carbon-cycle variability changes ^14C gradually. In 2012, Fusa Miyake and colleagues reported something different: Japanese cedar rings showed an approximately 12‰ jump from 774 to 775 CE, far sharper than ordinary solar modulation. A second rapid event around 993–994 CE was reported in 2013.

Subsequent multi-isotope work found corresponding signals in cosmogenic isotopes such as ^10Be and ^36Cl, strongly supporting extreme solar energetic particle events as the source of the best-established spikes.

Sources:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11123
- https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2783
- https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms9611

The term “Miyake event” is now commonly used for rapid, unusually large cosmogenic-radiocarbon excursions of this kind.

A 2024 Nature perspective listed five well-confirmed extreme solar-particle events at 7176 BCE, 5259 BCE, ~660 BCE, 774 CE and 993 CE, plus additional candidates requiring different levels of confirmation. The exact list continues to evolve as annual-resolution ^14C datasets expand.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07679-4

## 2. Why archaeologists care

Ordinary radiocarbon dating rarely returns one exact calendar year. The atmospheric calibration curve contains plateaus and wiggles, measurement uncertainty remains, and an archaeological wood sample may itself contain many annual rings.

A globally recognizable rapid ^14C excursion changes the problem.

If an undated wood sequence contains the same distinctive excursion as an absolutely dated master chronology, the event provides an anchor. Once the event ring or event pattern is identified, dendrochronologists can count annual rings forward or backward to a felling edge or another archaeological point.

The event therefore functions more like a synchronization marker than an ordinary radiocarbon age estimate.

### L'Anse aux Meadows

A 2022 Nature paper used the 993 CE radiocarbon excursion in three pieces of worked wood from L'Anse aux Meadows. After identifying the event and counting rings outward to the bark edge, the authors concluded that the trees were cut in 1021 CE.

This established a precise year in which Norse activity occurred at the site. It does **not** mean the entire settlement existed only in 1021 or that this was necessarily the first European arrival in the Americas. It provides a secure activity date.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8

### The Changbaishan / Paektu Millennium Eruption

A tree killed by the eruption contained the 774–775 CE spike. Counting outward from the spike to the tree's final ring placed the eruption in 946 CE; later work reproduced the spike in another tree. That resolved a long-standing chronology problem and ruled out historical interpretations requiring an eruption decades earlier.

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277379116305017
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/72CE2CE847E3A01C0858B82487039C7D/S0033822217000753a.pdf

### Neolithic Dispilio

A 2024 Nature Communications study identified the 5259 BCE event in a floating 303-year juniper chronology from the waterlogged Neolithic settlement at Dispilio in northern Greece. That allowed the chronology's final ring to be placed at 5140 BCE and anchored associated archaeological material to absolute calendar years.

This matters because floating dendrochronologies can have exquisite *relative* year-by-year structure without knowing where they sit on the absolute calendar. The cosmogenic event can supply that missing coordinate.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48402-1

## 3. The first correction: an “exact-year date” does not mean the solar event itself is always recorded in one exact ring everywhere

The method's success can create a misleading mental model:

`solar event occurs → atmosphere jumps → every tree records the same jump in the same annual ring`

That is too simple.

The radiocarbon first has to be produced and mixed through the atmosphere. The tree then samples atmospheric CO₂ only while photosynthesizing. The carbon used to build a particular part of a ring can come partly from current photosynthesis and partly from carbohydrates stored in previous years.

Tree rings are therefore biological proxies, not direct atmospheric instruments.

The ~660 BCE event demonstrates the issue clearly. A 2024 Communications Earth & Environment study found its signal extended across multiple years in different records and constrained the event to 664–663 BCE. The authors explicitly cautioned against treating that event as a straightforward single-year dating marker.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x

## 4. The 2026 tree-physiology result changes what the ring means

Hessl and colleagues' 2026 New Phytologist synthesis/modeling work attacks a usually implicit assumption: that annual tree-ring ^14C is a faithful annual sample of tropospheric ^14CO₂.

Their comparison of the 774 event across tree groups shows systematic differences:

- deciduous conifers such as larch can show an earlier rise and strong peak;
- deciduous angiosperms such as oak can show a delayed rise and peak;
- evergreen conifers can produce a broader, lower response.

Source: https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.70868

Three mechanisms matter.

### Photosynthetic season

Trees do not take up atmospheric carbon uniformly throughout the year. Growing-season length varies with species, latitude, temperature, moisture and year. A radiocarbon pulse occurring late in the growing season can therefore be sampled very differently from the same pulse occurring before or during peak photosynthesis.

### Wood formation is not the same thing as photosynthesis

A tree may stop forming new wood before it stops photosynthesizing, or begin forming earlywood before substantial current-year photosynthesis. This determines which ring receives newly produced atmospheric carbon.

### Stored non-structural carbohydrates

Trees store carbon and reuse it later. The 2026 paper summarizes experimental evidence that some earlywood contains substantial carbon assimilated in previous years. In cited subsection measurements, roughly half of oak earlywood carbon and roughly a quarter of larch earlywood carbon could derive from earlier assimilation, while latewood relied more strongly on current-year carbon.

In the authors' simple models, stored carbon can blur a sharp atmospheric Miyake signal over several years and shift the apparent peak. The effect is stronger for a modeled deciduous tree with a larger, slower-turnover carbon store.

This is not evidence that Miyake dating “doesn't work.” It explains why **event matching should use replicated signatures, appropriate species/tissue choices, earlywood/latewood separation where necessary, and cross-dating rather than assuming one globally identical spike ring**.

---

## 5. A useful distinction: dating the wood versus reconstructing the solar event

The physiology correction matters differently for two goals.

### Goal A: place archaeological wood on the calendar

If a known event produces a distinctive multi-year pattern and the wood sequence has enough annual rings, matching that pattern can still anchor the chronology. Once anchored, ring counting can give an exact felling year even if the atmospheric production event itself was spread or biologically blurred in the tree record.

This is what makes the L'Anse aux Meadows and Dispilio results powerful.

### Goal B: infer exactly when and how large the solar particle event was

Here, biological blurring is a larger problem. If different species integrate atmospheric ^14C differently, the tree-ring peak cannot be treated as a direct measurement of the instantaneous atmospheric production pulse.

The 2026 work therefore weakens naive solar-event reconstruction more than it weakens careful archaeological synchronization.

That distinction is central to the public version.

## 6. The discovery itself had an instructive correction

The 2013 paper reporting the 993–994 event later required a corrigendum because a missing tree-ring layer had shifted dates by one year in the original count.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms3747

That is a small historical detail but a useful one. A method capable of producing exact-year anchors is still only as exact as its tree-ring chronology, sample preparation, cross-dating and reference chronology.

The precision is earned through multiple independent constraints; it is not inherent in the existence of a radiocarbon spike.

## 7. Why this capability appeared recently

Miyake events existed in the archive all along. Two changes made them useful.

### Annual-resolution radiocarbon measurement

Older calibration datasets often measured blocks of 5, 10 or 20 rings. A one-year spike can be diluted or disappear inside such averaging.

Accelerator mass spectrometry has become efficient enough to measure very small carbon samples at high precision, enabling large annual-resolution campaigns.

The 2024 Nature perspective explicitly connects modern high-precision AMS with the rapid expansion of annually resolved ^14C series.

### Search after vocabulary existed

Once the 774–775 anomaly had been recognized as a distinct class, researchers could deliberately search for similar patterns across other chronologies and periods.

This is a good example of a measurement discovery creating both a new natural-hazard record and a new archaeological dating tool.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07679-4

## 8. What not every archaeological site can do

The method has demanding prerequisites.

The material must contain annual growth structure that can be measured reliably. Enough rings must survive to search for a known event pattern. The approximate age must usually be constrained enough that researchers know which event to test. Missing rings, false rings, reused timber, absent bark edges and species-specific growth behavior can complicate the final felling date.

The known Miyake events are also sparse. They are powerful anchors when a chronology crosses one, not a universal replacement for radiocarbon calibration or dendrochronology.

This is why the best description is **astrochronological tie-point**, not magical exact-date radiocarbon.

---

## 9. What the research changed

The starting candidate was: “solar storms leave exact-year barcodes in trees, letting archaeologists date wood precisely.”

Three corrections improved it.

1. **The event is an anchor, not the whole dating method.** The precision comes from spike recognition plus dendrochronological ring counting and archaeological context.
2. **Different trees can record the same atmospheric event differently.** Species, latitude, phenology, earlywood/latewood formation and stored carbon can shift or blur the annual signal.
3. **That does not erase exact-year archaeology.** A reproducible multi-year signature can still place a floating chronology on the absolute calendar even when the tree is an imperfect atmospheric recorder.

The final question is therefore more interesting than “can a solar storm date a Viking site?”:

> **How can a biological archive be precise enough to anchor history to a year while still being physiologically imperfect as a recorder of the physical event that created the anchor?**

---

## 10. Provisional conclusion

Miyake events have already produced genuine calendar-year archaeological results. The 993 event anchored worked wood from L'Anse aux Meadows to 1021 CE; the 774 event helped secure the 946 CE date of the Changbaishan eruption; the 5259 BCE event anchored a European Neolithic tree-ring chronology.

But a tree ring is not a one-year atmospheric sampling vial. New 2026 work shows that carbon uptake, wood formation and stored carbohydrates can blur or shift a radiocarbon pulse differently across species and environments.

The capability survives because archaeology often needs a recognizable temporal fingerprint, not a perfect reconstruction of the solar particle pulse. The physiology frontier tells researchers how to use the fingerprint without confusing the proxy with the event itself.
