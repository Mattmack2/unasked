**Novelty:** `KNOWN FIELD`

## Validated answer

Rare, well-characterized cosmogenic-radiocarbon events can genuinely anchor an otherwise floating annual tree-ring sequence to the calendar. That capability has already produced exact-year archaeological and geological results: three worked wood pieces at L'Anse aux Meadows converge on cutting in 1021 CE; the Changbaishan/Paektu Millennium Eruption is securely placed in late 946 CE; and a 303-year Neolithic juniper chronology from Dispilio is anchored so its final ring falls in 5140 BCE.

The important correction is that **the precision belongs to the entire inference chain, not to a magical one-ring spike**. A useful tie point requires a replicated event fingerprint, a trustworthy annual chronology, sufficiently resolved radiocarbon measurements, and ring counting to the archaeological or natural target.

Tree physiology also matters more than the naive version of the story admits. Empirical comparisons of the 774 CE event show systematic differences among tree groups: larch records rise earlier and peak in 775 CE, whereas oak records rise later and peak in 776 CE; evergreen conifers are intermediate. Modern isotope studies also show that some earlywood is built partly from carbon assimilated in previous years. Hessl et al. (2026) synthesize these results and model how stored non-structural carbohydrates, photosynthetic season and wood-formation timing can blur or shift a sharp atmospheric pulse.

That does **not** invalidate exact-year archaeology. A biological proxy can distort the detailed shape of a physical event and still preserve a distinctive enough fingerprint to synchronize an annual archive. The physiology problem is more damaging when researchers try to reconstruct the exact onset, seasonal timing or atmospheric magnitude of the solar event, and when a candidate event is weak or prolonged rather than a large, globally replicated fingerprint.

## Claim ledger

### 1. Miyake events can provide annual calendar anchors

**Status: survives strongly, with an important wording correction.**

A 2024 Nature perspective describes extreme solar-particle-event radiocarbon signatures as a route to annual-precision dating of suitable organic material. The strongest older global test is Büntgen et al. (2018): 484 individual rings from 44 tree-ring records on five continents showed globally coherent 774 and 993 CE excursions and independently validated the precise dating of the participating chronologies.

This is stronger than ordinary radiocarbon dating because the distinctive annual pattern acts as a synchronization marker. Once the pattern is placed in an annual tree-ring sequence, counting rings can transfer that calendar position to a bark edge, felling edge, eruption-killed outer ring or other target.

Sources:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07679-4
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-06036-0

### 2. The 774/775 and 993/994 signals are securely linked to extreme solar-particle events

**Status: survives for these flagship events; do not generalize to every proposed excursion.**

Mekhaldi et al. (2015) found matching annual ^10Be anomalies in Arctic and Antarctic ice cores and used ^36Cl as an additional energy-sensitive cosmogenic isotope. The multi-radionuclide pattern supports an extreme solar-proton origin for the 774/775 and 993/994 events and rules out the leading non-solar explanations considered in that analysis.

But `rapid radiocarbon excursion` should not be treated as synonymous with `confirmed solar event`. Some proposed excursions fail independent replication, and the origin of all recognized events is not equally constrained.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms9611

### 3. L'Anse aux Meadows is exactly dated to 1021 CE

**Status: survives, narrowed to what was actually dated.**

Kuitems et al. measured rings near the 993 anomaly in three worked wood pieces with preserved waney edges. All three independently converged on a cutting year of 1021 CE. The preserved edges and tool-modified wood make later driftwood reuse implausible, and the authors conclude that Norse people were active at L'Anse aux Meadows in that year.

What does **not** follow is that 1021 was necessarily the first Norse arrival, the founding year of the settlement, or the only year of occupation. The secure claim is European/Norse presence and wood-working activity in 1021.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03972-8

### 4. Changbaishan/Paektu's 946 CE date does not rest on one fragile spike match

**Status: survives more strongly after validation.**

Oppenheimer et al. (2017) identified the 774/775 cosmogenic radiocarbon signal in a subfossil larch killed by the Millennium Eruption, then combined the tree-ring chronology with Greenland ice-core glaciochemistry to date the eruption to late 946 CE.

An independent tree subsequently reproduced the 774/775 spike and again placed its outermost ring in 946 CE. Later reassessment continues to treat 946 CE as the secure cosmogenic tree-ring date, consistent with a contemporary Japanese chronicle reporting ash fallout in early November 946.

This is a useful example of the correct epistemic structure: the radiocarbon event is a tie point inside a multi-proxy chronology, not a standalone magic number.

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277379116305017
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0377027323000446

### 5. The Dispilio Neolithic chronology survives missing-ring concerns

**Status: survives, with explicit methodological constraints.**

Maczkowski et al. (2024) anchored a 303-year juniper chronology from the waterlogged Neolithic site of Dispilio using the 5259 BCE event. The event was sought after an initial conventional wiggle match, then identified through **115 single-ring measurements in four separately crossdated juniper samples**. The same increase appeared in the same relative ring across the samples, placing the final juniper ring at 5140 BCE.

The paper explicitly discusses missing and false rings. Juniper can be difficult because lobate growth can produce locally absent rings, but the study used complete cross-sections where possible, replicated tree-ring sequences, a master chronology, crossdating to oak, and repeated radiocarbon detection across multiple samples. The risk is therefore real but not ignored.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48402-1

### 6. A Miyake event is not always a one-ring, one-year marker

**Status: Turn 1 correction strengthened.**

The ca. 660 BCE event is the cleanest counterexample. A 2024 Communications Earth & Environment study constrained its main production episode to 664–663 BCE but found a prolonged radiocarbon signature, including an approximately 12‰ rise over two years, and explicitly cautioned against using it naively for precise single-year dating.

This means the useful object is not necessarily `the spike ring`. It can be a **multi-year radiocarbon fingerprint** whose placement is constrained by replication, shape and dendrochronology.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x

### 7. Tree physiology can shift and blur the apparent event signal

**Status: survives strongly and is empirically grounded, not only modeled.**

Hessl et al. (2026) compared published 774 CE records by functional group. Ten Larix deciduous-conifer series show an early rise in 774 and a peak in 775. Three Quercus deciduous-angiosperm series show a delayed rise in 775 and peak in 776. Thirty-three evergreen-conifer series show an intermediate rise and lower peak.

The same synthesis reviews independent isotope experiments showing why. Kromer et al.'s modern bomb-spike work inferred that about 50% of the carbon used in Quercus earlywood and about 25% in Larix earlywood had been assimilated in previous years, while latewood was predominantly current-year carbon. Other studies have found approximately one-year bulk-ring offsets in some species, although counterexamples also exist.

Hessl et al.'s simple models then show the expected consequence for a sharp radiocarbon pulse: stored carbon can flatten the peak, carry the signal into later growth and shift which annual ring appears most enriched. Their point is not that all published event chronologies are wrong. It is that a tree ring cannot automatically be treated as a direct one-year atmospheric sampling vial.

Sources:
- https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.70868
- https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.71002

### 8. Earlywood/latewood separation is important, but not a universal prerequisite for every archaeological use

**Status: corrected.**

The 2026 physiology synthesis argues strongly for distinguishing earlywood and latewood when the goal is to reconstruct past atmospheric composition or infer the detailed timing and magnitude of an abrupt event. It also shows why subannual work can improve event interpretation.

But successful archaeological tie-point studies have already used whole annual rings when the event fingerprint is sufficiently strong and the chronology is independently controlled. Dispilio, for example, used whole juniper rings and reproduced the event in four samples.

So the practical hierarchy is:

`subannual anatomy and chemistry are increasingly important for proxy reconstruction`

but

`they are not a retroactive requirement for every robust annual synchronization result`.

Sources:
- https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nph.70868
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48402-1

### 9. The number of “known Miyake events” should not be frozen into a simple public count

**Status: Turn 1 wording corrected.**

Different papers count only strongly confirmed extreme solar-particle events, include additional rapid radiocarbon excursions, or discuss candidates at different validation stages. A 2024 study described six documented events, while the 2024 Nature perspective discusses a larger and evolving literature of confirmed events and additional excursions.

The public article should therefore say **a small set of well-characterized or replicated events** and name the event being used, rather than assert that there are exactly five, six or seven Miyake events.

Sources:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01618-x
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07679-4

### 10. Replication is a real filter, not ceremonial caution

**Status: important negative evidence.**

A proposed rapid excursion around 3372–3371 BCE was originally reported from a Chinese wingnut tree. Independent measurements in an absolutely dated bristlecone pine from California and a European oak did not reproduce the proposed spike. The authors concluded that the event could not be confirmed without resolving the original chronology.

That failed replication is useful because it shows why `globally recognizable event` is an earned status. The method depends on independent crossdating and replication precisely because individual tree physiology, local chronology and laboratory work can all create ambiguity.

Source:
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09387

## Strongest negative result

The ca. 660 BCE event is more important to the public story than a generic methods caveat. It is a real cosmogenic event with a strong radiocarbon signature, yet its signal is prolonged enough that the researchers explicitly warn against treating it as a simple single-year stamp.

That breaks the attractive but wrong model:

`solar storm → one universal spike ring → exact date`

The better model is:

`physical production event`
`→ atmospheric transport and carbon-cycle response`
`→ species-specific uptake/storage/wood formation`
`→ measured annual or subannual fingerprint`
`→ replicated crossdated match`
`→ calendar anchor`

Exact archaeology appears at the end of that chain.

## What changed in Turn 2

Turn 1 began with a useful tension: a tree can apparently provide a one-year historical timestamp even though it is a living carbon-allocation system rather than an atmospheric instrument.

Validation made that tension sharper rather than dissolving it.

First, the archaeological capability became more secure. L'Anse is based on three separate trees. Dispilio reproduced the event across four samples and 115 individual rings while explicitly controlling missing-ring problems. Changbaishan combines a killed tree, a cosmogenic anchor, ice-core evidence and independent second-tree replication.

Then the physiology correction also became stronger. The 2026 paper does not merely speculate that trees *might* blur a signal; the 774 datasets already show systematic functional-group offsets, and independent isotope work demonstrates substantial use of older stored carbon in earlywood for some species.

The resulting mental model is better than either extreme. A tree does not need to record the sky perfectly to synchronize history precisely. It needs to preserve a sufficiently distinctive, independently validated temporal fingerprint inside a trustworthy annual sequence.

## Verdict

`SURVIVES_WITH_CORRECTIONS`.

The strongest public story is no longer “solar storms leave exact-year barcodes in trees.” It is more precise and stranger:

**A tree can date history more precisely than it records the sky.**

A well-replicated cosmogenic fingerprint can synchronize an annual biological archive to a calendar year even though atmospheric transport, phenology and stored carbon distort the detailed physical signal. Exactness is therefore not a property of one anomalous ring. It is the result of several independent constraints agreeing on where that fingerprint belongs.
