## Re-anchored question

**Can a smell meaningfully be preserved as cultural heritage—and if the physical source or living practice disappears, what exactly can an archive save?**

## Validated answer

**Yes, if “preserve” is unpacked.** Olfactory heritage is now a real interdisciplinary field with peer-reviewed methods, heritage toolkits, digital infrastructure, and physical reference collections. Some bounded historical smells can also be reproduced with surprisingly rigorous chemical and sensory methods.

But the durable archive is usually not one frozen smell formula. It is a layered record:

```text
material source or living practice
+ chemical evidence
+ perceptual evidence
+ historical context
+ cultural meaning
+ reconstruction method
+ explicit temporal target
```

That model survives Turn 2 much better than “science can bottle the past.”

## Claim ledger

### 1. Is olfactory heritage a real field?

Yes. Bembibre and Strlič proposed a structured framework for identifying, analysing and archiving historic odours in 2017. A 2022 review describes an interdisciplinary field spanning chemistry, history, archaeology, anthropology, sensory science, museology and heritage practice. Odeuropa later produced an encyclopedia, smell-history explorer, semantic model, museum storytelling toolkit, policy toolkit and Heritage Smell Library. UNESCO highlighted the preservation problem in July 2026 while stressing that smell remains largely neglected in standard heritage practice.

**Verdict:** survives. Do not confuse emerging recognition with a new formal UNESCO category.

### 2. Can a historical smell be reproduced at the perceptual level?

The strongest evidence is Paolin et al. (npj Heritage Science, June 2026). The team analysed an early-20th-century perfume using GC-MS with olfactory detection, ranked odor-active compounds, selected 13 compounds important to the perceived profile, used omission testing, and compared the reconstruction with the surviving sample using 70 trained and untrained panellists. They reported no significant sensory difference under the experiment.

The limitation is crucial: they targeted the **current smell of the aged sample**. This was not recovery of the perfume's smell when new, and a bounded flask is much simpler than a market, factory, ritual or city smellscape.

**Verdict:** survives with scope correction.

### 3. Is chemistry alone enough?

No. Odor perception is nonlinear with concentration; some trace compounds dominate because their human detection thresholds are extremely low. The 2026 perfume work needed olfactory detection, odor-activity values, omission tests and sensory assessment rather than ordinary chemical profiling alone. Heritage frameworks also pair instrumental measurements with sensory and historical documentation.

**Verdict:** survives.

### 4. How culturally variable is smell?

Turn 2 found useful counterevidence to any simple “smell is culturally constructed” story. A 2022 Current Biology study found substantial cross-cultural agreement in pleasantness rankings for monomolecular odorants, with molecular identity explaining much more variance than culture. A 2024 cross-cultural study also found broad similarities but meaningful differences; familiarity was a strong predictor of pleasantness and some populations diverged substantially.

The correct conclusion is mixed: human olfaction has shared biological structure, while exposure, familiarity, language, context and personal history still shape experience and meaning.

**Verdict:** survives with balance.

### 5. Can reconstructions create false authenticity?

Yes, and the field is unusually explicit about it. The 2022 overview discusses authenticity tensions when synthetic scents enter museums. A 2026 Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology paper on the Scent of the Afterlife project says reconstructed archaeological scents are historically informed interpretations, not complete recoveries. Ancient materials have changed, modern ingredients and safety rules differ, concentration information may be missing, and a perfumer necessarily makes choices.

**Verdict:** survives and should be central to the published article.

### 6. Is “which temporal version?” a new insight?

No. Conservation theory has long distinguished original appearance, present material state, patina, later alteration and historically significant ageing. Brandi-derived conservation thinking explicitly warns against erasing evidence of time or creating a false historical state.

Olfactory heritage makes the problem vivid because ageing literally changes the volatile molecules being smelled, but the underlying conservation question is mature.

**Verdict:** novelty claim rejected; useful mental model retained.

### 7. What does a smell library really preserve?

Odeuropa's Heritage Smell Library began with 19 reference scents stored at the Osmothèque. Some are single significant materials; others are reconstructions based on chemical evidence or recreations based on recipes and historical research. Crucially, Odeuropa says the library also preserves detailed documentation of cultural significance and development method.

Turn 2 did not find good evidence that a stored scent vial remains chemically and sensorially invariant for many decades. The safer model is **sample + recipe/method + provenance + interpretation**, not an immutable olfactory object.

**Verdict:** survives with correction.

## Strongest synthesis

The easiest story was “we can now recreate lost smells.” The better story is about archival bias.

Museums are designed around things that sit still. Smell does not. A place's smell may be produced by materials, people, weather, food, fuel, animals, microbes, ventilation and practice. The physical source changes as it ages. Even a scientifically exact reconstruction of an object's present odour can be wrong for its original odour.

This means olfactory preservation often resembles ordinary conservation more than media recording. A painting can have original pigment, later varnish, fading, restoration and patina. A smell can have original ingredients, degradation products, environmental mixing, lost materials, changed concentrations and new social associations.

The archive becomes trustworthy when it says **which historical state it is trying to represent and which parts were measured versus inferred.**

## Strongest practical consequence

Turn 2 strengthens a more useful strategy than perfect future reconstruction: **document vulnerable smellscapes while they still exist.**

UNESCO's 2026 feature describes work around Bahia's Lavagem do Bonfim that pairs chemistry with practitioners' descriptions to create a baseline of what the aromatic practice smells like now. That record can later reveal what changed as plant availability, climate, materials or transmission change.

The question shifts from:

> How do we resurrect a smell after it disappears?

into:

> Which sensory dimensions of a living practice should we document before we realize they mattered?
