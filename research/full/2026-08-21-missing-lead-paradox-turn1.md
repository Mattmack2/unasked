## Pareto selection

C03 was selected.

It adds a sixth primary domain to the current publication trajectory if it eventually survives, but domain balance was not enough by itself. The stronger reason is that the vocabulary changed the question immediately.

“Missing lead” sounds like Earth is short some kilograms of Pb.

The **missing lead paradox** is stranger: the isotopic composition of the lead we can sample does not add up cleanly under a standard model of Earth's origin and differentiation. The discrepancy lets geochemists reason about a reservoir they may never have directly sampled.

A February 2026 Nature Communications paper then supplied a new physical mechanism: high-pressure Pb-S compounds that could sequester old, unradiogenic lead in the deep mantle.

---

# 1. The simple version

Lead has several stable isotopes.

One of them, **lead-204**, is not produced by radioactive decay. Three others include lead-206 and lead-207, which are produced over geological time by the decay of uranium-238 and uranium-235, plus lead-208 from thorium-232.

That makes lead an unusually useful clock and tracer.

If a rock reservoir spends billions of years with a high uranium-to-lead ratio, it gradually develops more **radiogenic lead**. If lead is separated away from uranium, its isotope composition remains comparatively **unradiogenic**.

Geochemists can therefore use ratios such as `206Pb/204Pb` and `207Pb/204Pb` to reconstruct long-term separation and mixing between Earth's reservoirs.

The paradox is that much of the accessible crust and mantle is **too radiogenic** relative to a simple model in which Earth began with a chondritic, meteorite-like lead composition and its silicate reservoirs merely redistributed material internally.

If the known reservoirs are all biased to one side of the isotope balance, then at least one of these must be true:

1. a complementary reservoir of old, unradiogenic lead is hidden somewhere we rarely sample;
2. some lead was transferred to another major reservoir such as the core;
3. Earth's starting composition was not as chondritic as the simple model assumes;
4. the standard isotope-evolution model is missing important differentiation, recycling or mixing history.

So the “missing” object is really **the complementary isotope history**.

---

# 2. Why this is interesting

The initial attraction was the hidden-reservoir idea: isotope ratios apparently pointing toward material thousands of kilometres below us.

The research made the story less tidy and more interesting.

The paradox is not one mysterious clue awaiting one secret chamber. It has survived because several parts of the model can move at once: Earth's starting composition, the timing of crust–mantle differentiation, lead loss during core formation, hydrothermal alteration of oceanic crust, sulfur chemistry, subduction, and the fact that volcanic rocks do not sample the mantle uniformly.

That means the 2026 result is valuable precisely because it is **not** “we found the missing lead.”

It says: if the missing-reservoir version of the problem is real, here is a concrete way deep mantle mineral physics could make that reservoir physically stable for billions of years.

The larger mental-model change is methodological:

> **planetary composition can be constrained by what the samples fail to balance, not only by what has been directly recovered.**

# 3. Vocabulary graft

## Radiogenic lead

Lead isotopes produced by radioactive decay. `206Pb` grows from `238U`, `207Pb` from `235U`, and `208Pb` from `232Th`.

## Unradiogenic / common lead

Lead that contains a larger relative contribution from isotope components not produced by later uranium/thorium decay. `204Pb` is the standard non-radiogenic reference isotope.

## U–Th–Pb system

The coupled uranium, thorium and lead isotope system used for both geochronology and tracing long-term planetary differentiation.

## Chondrite

A primitive stony meteorite. Chondritic compositions are often used as reference approximations for Solar System starting material, although Earth is not chemically identical to any one chondrite class.

## Bulk silicate Earth (BSE)

Earth's mantle plus crust, excluding the metallic core.

## Geochron

In classic Pb-isotope diagrams, the meteorite isochron corresponding to Solar System age. Under a simple chondritic closed-system model, bulk terrestrial lead evolution should balance around it.

## First lead paradox / first Pb paradox

The observation that much accessible terrestrial lead plots more radiogenically than expected, implying a complementary unradiogenic reservoir or shortcomings in assumptions about Earth's starting composition/evolution.

## HIMU

A mantle geochemistry term for sources with high time-integrated `238U/204Pb` (high μ), producing very radiogenic lead isotope signatures. It is part of the broader “mantle zoo” used to classify source components sampled by ocean-island basalts.

## Chalcophile

An element with chemical affinity for sulfur. Lead can partition into sulfide phases under appropriate redox and chemical conditions.

## Peridotite

A magnesium- and iron-rich rock characteristic of Earth's upper mantle.

# 4. Genealogy: lead helped date Earth, then became a bookkeeping problem

The genealogy runs through one of geochemistry's most famous achievements.

Clair Patterson's 1956 lead-isotope work on meteorites produced a precise age for the Solar System and Earth of about 4.55 billion years. The basic idea was that uranium decay and lead isotope evolution give a long-term clock when the starting and reservoir relationships are constrained.

Source:
- https://courses.seas.harvard.edu/climate/eli/Courses/EPS281r/Sources/Earth-age-and-thermal-history/more/Patterson-1956-meteorites-and-age-of-earth.pdf

By the late 1960s, geochemists noticed an awkward feature: most sampled terrestrial mantle and crustal materials carried lead isotope ratios that were too radiogenic to balance a simple chondritic silicate Earth. Allègre's 1969 work is usually cited as an early formulation of the first lead paradox.

The problem persisted through decades of increasingly sophisticated mantle models.

A 2008 Nature Geoscience commentary described it as **the enduring lead paradox**: known rock reservoirs were collectively too radiogenic, implying some complementary reservoir or a flaw in the model.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo372

That is already a good scientific story: one isotope system helps establish Earth's age, then the same system tells us our inventory of Earth's internal reservoirs is incomplete.

---

# 5. The empirical clues: unradiogenic lead does exist in mantle material

The hidden reservoir is not purely mathematical.

In 2008, researchers reported extremely unradiogenic lead isotope ratios from peridotites in Japan's **Horoman massif**, interpreted as ancient depleted mantle domains that had escaped full convective homogenization. The paper suggested that similar domains, if sufficiently abundant, could supply a complementary unradiogenic reservoir.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo363

That result immediately created another question: if such material is common enough to balance the planet's Pb isotope budget, why do ordinary mantle-derived magmas almost never sample it?

A 2012 Nature Geoscience study found another clue in abyssal peridotites from the Atlantic Ocean floor. Sulfide inclusions trapped inside silicate minerals preserved extremely unradiogenic lead. The authors argued that shielding inside silicates or residence in refractory mantle domains could prevent this component from contributing strongly to normal oceanic basalts.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo1531

That matters for the 2026 story because it connects “lead likes sulfur” to actual mantle samples rather than leaving sulfur sequestration as a purely theoretical convenience.

---

# 6. Older solutions: put it in the core, move it through crust, or change the model

There has never been one accepted solution.

## Core sequestration

Because lead can behave as a chalcophile/siderophile element under some conditions, one class of models places missing unradiogenic Pb in the metallic core or in sulfide segregating during core formation.

But experimental and modeling studies have questioned whether the known partitioning and timing can move enough lead into the core to solve the full isotope balance.

A 2008 Nature paper was titled bluntly: **The Earth's missing lead may not be in the core.**

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07375

## Oceanic-crust cycling

Another family of explanations focuses on preferential lead mobility during seafloor alteration and dehydration of subducting oceanic crust. Because U and Pb do not move identically, long-term plate recycling can generate radiogenic and unradiogenic source domains without requiring one simple static reservoir.

Source:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0009254195001033

## Change the whole isotope-evolution framework

A 2023 Earth-Science Reviews synthesis used modern global datasets to argue that the first Pb paradox can be substantially reinterpreted through differentiated Pb evolution paths, source mixing, gravitational differentiation and subduction-led remixing. Their framework reduces the need to treat every right-of-geochron sample as proof of one enormous hidden complementary reservoir.

Source:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012825223001721

This is a critical Turn 2 target. The 2026 hidden-reservoir mechanism is not automatically the solution merely because it is physically plausible.

# 7. The 2026 mechanism: pressure can make lead-sulfur storage much harder to melt

The February 2026 Nature Communications paper asks a more specific question:

**Could lead form sulfur-bearing minerals that remain stable under the pressures and temperatures of the deep mantle, physically separating it from uranium and thorium for geological time?**

The authors used first-principles crystal-structure prediction and melting calculations to map high-pressure Pb-S phases.

They report several relevant behaviors:

### PbS

Ordinary lead sulfide—galena at crustal conditions—changes crystal structure under pressure. Their calculations indicate high-pressure PbS remains refractory enough to stay solid through mantle conditions up toward the core–mantle boundary.

If PbS crystallized during early Earth differentiation or later sulfide segregation, it could lock Pb into a deep reservoir while U and Th, which are strongly lithophile, followed different mineral/melting paths.

### PbS2

The study predicts pressure-stabilized PbS2 phases that can remain crystalline under portions of upper/deep mantle conditions, offering another possible Pb host in sulfur-rich regions.

### PbS3

PbS3 behaves differently: its lower predicted melting point could allow some Pb to become mobile. As material rises and pressure drops, the phase could destabilize and release a small amount of old unradiogenic lead back toward the accessible mantle.

That gives the model both **storage and leakage**.

A perfectly isolated reservoir would explain the missing component but struggle to explain rare unradiogenic signatures that do appear in upper-mantle samples. A mostly stable reservoir with occasional remobilization is therefore more interesting.

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-69772-8

# 8. What I initially thought the 2026 paper had done—and what it actually did

The first impression was stronger than the evidence:

> scientists found the mineral reservoir containing Earth's missing lead.

They did not.

The work is primarily **computational mineral physics**. It predicts crystal structures, thermodynamic stability and melting behavior at pressures relevant to Earth's mantle. The authors then connect those results to known lead-isotope observations and sulfur cycling.

No drill core from the lower mantle contains the proposed global PbS reservoir. We cannot drill thousands of kilometres into the mantle, and most of what we know about it comes from seismic observations, high-pressure experiments, calculations and material transported upward by mantle processes.

So the current result is better described as:

> **a plausible physical mechanism now exists for a class of hidden-reservoir solutions that previously lacked a clear deep-mantle mineral host.**

Turn 2 must preserve that boundary aggressively.

---

# 9. Why pressure matters

This was the less obvious conceptual connection.

A material that melts, reacts or dissolves readily near the surface can behave very differently under tens or hundreds of gigapascals of pressure.

High pressure changes crystal structure, bonding and melting temperature. A chemical phase that would not seem like a durable planetary vault at surface conditions can become stable deep inside a planet.

That means “where is the missing element?” is sometimes inseparable from “what phases are even stable at the pressure where it might be hiding?”

This is one reason deep-Earth chemistry is not just ordinary mineralogy extrapolated downward.

---

# 10. The hidden-reservoir idea has a sampling problem

If a huge unradiogenic reservoir occupies the mantle, why do mid-ocean ridge basalts and ocean-island basalts mostly fail to reveal it?

There are several possible answers, none yet decisive:

- the relevant material may be physically deep and rarely entrained into common melts;
- sulfide grains could remain in refractory residues while silicate melts escape;
- inclusions inside silicate minerals can protect ancient sulfide Pb from equilibration;
- the reservoir may be spatially patchy rather than one continuous layer;
- the entire mass-balance assumption may overstate how large a hidden reservoir is required.

The Horoman and abyssal-peridotite results show that unusually unradiogenic mantle Pb can avoid ordinary basaltic sampling. They do not establish the global volume needed to close the budget.

---

# 11. What is established vs inferred

## FOUNDATION / ESTABLISHED

- U and Th decay generate radiogenic Pb isotopes over geological time.
- Pb isotope ratios are major tracers of crust–mantle differentiation and planetary history.
- Under classical chondritic/simple reservoir assumptions, much accessible terrestrial Pb is too radiogenic: the first Pb paradox.
- Extremely unradiogenic Pb has been measured in some mantle peridotites and mantle sulfide inclusions.
- Multiple competing explanations for the paradox have existed for decades.

## CURRENT / STRONG

- The 2026 calculations predict PbS, PbS2 and PbS3 phases with pressure-dependent stability relevant to mantle conditions.
- PbS is predicted to be refractory enough to provide long-lived deep storage under broad mantle conditions.
- PbS3's predicted lower melting behavior supplies a possible mechanism for limited remobilization.

## FRONTIER / INFERRED

- That these phases actually constitute a globally important hidden Pb reservoir.
- Their real abundance and spatial distribution in the lower mantle.
- Whether they close the quantitative Pb isotope mass balance better than alternative dynamic-Earth models.
- Whether a chondritic BSE reference is sufficiently accurate for the paradox to require a large hidden reservoir at all.

---

# 12. Strongest competing explanation

The strongest adversarial branch is not “the calculations are wrong.”

It is that the **problem may be over-formulated under an old global isotope model**.

The 2023 global lead-isotope review argues that modern databases and a differentiated, dynamically mixed Earth can largely mitigate the classical first and second Pb paradoxes without requiring one simple complementary reservoir. Under that view, shallow radiogenic reservoirs may be preferentially represented in the samples we collect, while source mixing and long-term differentiation move terrestrial data away from the naive geochron balance.

If that framework holds, the 2026 Pb-S phases may still be real and geochemically important without being “the solution to Earth's missing lead.”

That distinction should decide the Turn 2 verdict.

# 13. Cheap falsification / Turn 2 targets

Turn 2 must try to break at least these claims:

1. Is the first Pb paradox still considered a genuine mass-balance problem under current BSE and global-database estimates, or has newer modeling substantially dissolved it?
2. Is the 2026 Pb-S phase diagram supported by laboratory high-pressure data, or is it currently almost entirely first-principles prediction?
3. Are predicted Pb abundances and sulfur availability remotely sufficient to balance the missing unradiogenic component?
4. Could PbS realistically crystallize and remain isolated during magma-ocean/core-formation conditions, rather than simply being stable if artificially placed at depth?
5. Does mantle convection destroy the long-term isolation needed by the model?
6. Are observed unradiogenic Pb signatures in Horoman/abyssal peridotites quantitatively compatible with a large deep reservoir, or do they support only small refractory pockets?
7. Does the paper distinguish a mechanism that *could* host Pb from evidence that it *does* host the globally missing amount?
8. Which observable predictions could discriminate the pressure-stabilized-reservoir model from alternative dynamic-mixing models?

# 14. What changed during the research

1. **“Missing” stopped meaning missing mass.** The actual problem is isotope balance.
2. **The paradox became conditional.** A hidden reservoir is required most strongly under particular assumptions about starting composition and Earth evolution.
3. **The 2026 result got weaker in one sense and stronger in another.** It is not direct discovery, but it supplies a specific mineral-physics mechanism that can be experimentally attacked.
4. **Sulfur turned from side chemistry into the key separator.** Mantle sulfides had already preserved extremely unradiogenic Pb in real samples before the 2026 deep-mantle model.
5. **Sampling became part of the mechanism.** A reservoir can matter precisely because ordinary basalt-producing melting fails to sample it.
6. **The best future article may be about invisible planetary bookkeeping rather than lead itself.** Isotope systems can infer missing reservoirs because known reservoirs fail to sum correctly.

---

# 15. Provisional conclusion

The missing lead paradox is a real and historically important geochemical problem, but its strongest classical form depends on assumptions about chondritic starting composition and simplified reservoir evolution that modern frameworks continue to revise.

The 2026 Pb-S work is therefore best treated as a **new mechanism in an old argument**, not the final solution.

If it survives Turn 2, the reader-facing story should probably begin with the misconception built into the phrase “missing lead”: no one misplaced a layer of metal. The isotope ratios of the Earth we can touch imply that our inventory, our model of planetary evolution, or both are incomplete.

That is a much stranger way to discover something hidden.
